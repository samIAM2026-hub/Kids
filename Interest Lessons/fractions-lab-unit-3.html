0 Warm-up

What the two numbers actually mean

Every fraction is really a set of instructions. The bottom number tells you how many equal pieces the whole thing was cut into. The top number tells you how many of those pieces you are holding.

One whole chocolate bar, cut into 8 pieces. Take 3 of them → 38

2

The idea to hold onto
A bigger bottom number means smaller pieces.
112 is a smaller piece than 14.
One whole, in disguise A whole thing is all of its pieces. So 1 = 88 = 55 = 1212. You will use this trick constantly, because questions love to say "she started with 1 metre of ribbon."
1 Skill One

Adding and subtracting: the pieces must be the same size

You cannot add 3 apples and 5 oranges and call it 8 apples. In the same way you cannot add 12 and 16 until both are measured in the same size piece.

The rule
Same bottom number → add or subtract the tops only.
The bottom number never changes.

Make the bottoms match

Look at the two bottom numbers. Find a number that both of them go into. The fastest check: does the smaller one fit into the bigger one? If yes, you only have to change one fraction.

2 and 6 → 2 fits into 6, so use 6.

Rename the fraction (don't change its value)

Whatever you multiply the bottom by, multiply the top by the same number. The fraction looks different but is worth exactly the same.

½ → multiply top and bottom by 3 → 3/6

Add or subtract the top numbers

Now the pieces match, so just count them. Keep the bottom number the way it is.

3/6 + 1/6 = 4/6

Simplify if you can

If the top and bottom both divide by the same number, divide them both. 4/6 → divide both by 2 → 2/3. Always finish this step — it is where most marks are lost.

Worked example 2 — "how much longer?"

Worked example
A stick is 34 m long. A string is 512 m long. How much longer is the stick than the string?
1
"How much longer" always means subtract — the big one minus the small one.
34 − 512
2
Bottoms are 4 and 12. Does 4 fit into 12? Yes, 3 times. So rename quarters as twelfths — multiply top and bottom by 3.
34 = 3×34×3 = 912
3
Now subtract the tops: 9 − 5 = 4.
912 − 512 = 412
4
Simplify: 4 and 12 both divide by 4.
412 = 13
AnswerThe stick is 13 m longer.

Worked example 3 — "altogether"

Worked example
John spent 12 of his money on a toy car and 16 of his money on a pen. What fraction of his money did he spend altogether?
1
"Altogether" means add.
12 + 16
2
2 fits into 6 three times, so rename halves as sixths.
12 = 1×32×3 = 36
3
Add the tops: 3 + 1 = 4.
36 + 16 = 46
4
Simplify: both divide by 2.
46 = 23
AnswerHe spent 23 of his money.

Worked example 4 — the tricky "less than" one

Two-step · read carefully
Mary drank 310 litre of orange juice. Jim drank 15 litre less than Mary. How much orange juice did they drink altogether?
1
Spot the trap. The question does not tell you Jim's amount — it tells you how much smaller it is. So you must find Jim first, then add. Two steps, not one.
2
Jim = Mary − 15. Rename fifths as tenths (5 fits into 10 twice):
15 = 210
3
Now subtract to get Jim's amount.
310 − 210 = 110 litre
4
Altogether = Mary + Jim.
310 + 110 = 410 = 25
AnswerThey drank 25 litre altogether.

Worked example 5 — two things taken away from 1

Two-step
Lily bought 1 yd of ribbon. She used 12 yd to tie a package and 310 yd to make a bow. How much ribbon did she have left?
1
Find how much she used in total first. Rename halves as tenths:
12 = 510
2
Add the two amounts she used.
510 + 310 = 810
3
Take that away from the whole. Write 1 as tenths:
1 = 1010
4
Subtract, then simplify.
1010 − 810 = 210 = 15

2 Skill Two

Mixed numbers — whole ones plus a leftover

When you have more than one whole thing, you write it as a mixed number: the whole ones first, then the leftover fraction beside it. Three whole pizzas and half of a fourth pizza is 312.

How to read a picture
Count the complete shapes → that's the whole number.
Then look at the leftover shape: how many pieces is it cut into (bottom), how many are shaded (top)?

3 Skill Three

Improper fractions — count every piece

A mixed number describes the picture as "wholes and a bit." An improper fraction describes the exact same picture a different way: ignore the wholes, and just count how many little pieces there are in total.

The definition
An improper fraction has a top number bigger than or equal to its bottom number.
It is not a mistake. It just means "more than one whole."
Worked example
Two whole triangles, each cut into 3 equal parts. Write the improper fraction.
1
Each triangle gives 3 pieces, and there are 2 triangles. Count them: 3 + 3 = 6 pieces.
2
Every piece is a third, so the bottom number is 3. "6 thirds":
= 63
Answer63  (which is also 2 wholes)

Turning a mixed number into an improper fraction

The recipe
whole × bottom, then + top  →  that's your new top.
The bottom number stays exactly the same.
Worked example
Write 256 as an improper fraction.
1
Why the recipe works: each whole hexagon is 6 sixths. Two whole hexagons is 2 × 6 = 12 sixths.
2
Then add the 5 loose sixths you already had: 12 + 5 = 17.
3
The pieces are still sixths, so the bottom stays 6.
256 = 176
Answer176
The most common mistake in the whole unit Writing 256 as 256 by squashing the 2 and the 5 together. You must multiply then add — never just push the digits next to each other.

4 Skill Four

Improper fraction → mixed number

This is the reverse trip. You are asking: how many whole ones can I build out of these pieces, and what is left over? There are two ways to do it. Learn both — one is safer, one is faster.

Method 1 — the splitting method (what your book shows)

Method 1
Express 114 as a mixed number.
1
The pieces are quarters, so one whole = 4 quarters. Ask: what is the biggest multiple of 4 that fits inside 11? → 8.
2
Split the fraction at that point.
114 = 84 + 34
3
8 quarters is exactly 2 wholes.
= 2 + 34
4
Write it as a mixed number.
= 234
Answer234

Method 2 — the division method (faster once you trust it)

The recipe
Divide the top by the bottom.
The answer is the whole number · the remainder is the new top · the bottom stays the same.
Method 2
Express 185 as a mixed number.
1
18 ÷ 5. How many 5s fit in 18? Three, because 5 × 3 = 15.
2
What's left over? 18 − 15 = 3.
3
Whole number 3, remainder 3, bottom still 5.
185 = 335
Answer335
When the answer is a plain whole number If the division comes out with no remainder, there is no fraction part at all. 155 = 15 ÷ 5 = 3 exactly. Don't write 305 — just write 3.

5 Before the test

The four mistakes that cost the most marks

Adding the bottom numbers too

Writing 12 + 16 = 28. That answer is smaller than the fraction you started with, which is impossible when you're adding.

Fix: the bottom number is the size of the piece, not a count. It never changes when you add.

Stopping before simplifying

412 is right but unfinished. Teachers mark this as incomplete.

Fix: after every answer, ask "do the top and bottom both divide by 2? by 3? by 5?"

Treating "less than Mary" as the answer

Reading "Jim drank 15 litre less than Mary" as "Jim drank 15 litre."

Fix: underline the words less than. They mean you have a subtraction to do before the real question.

Squashing a mixed number's digits together

Turning 325 into 325.

Fix: say it out loud — "3 times 5 is 15, plus 2 is 17" → 175.
