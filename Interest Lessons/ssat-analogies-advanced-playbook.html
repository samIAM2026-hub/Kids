SSAT Verbal · Part Two

Analogies: The Advanced Playbook

The Method Lab taught you the bridge. This is everything that comes after the bridge — the moves that decide the hard questions, the rescue kit for words you don't know, twelve more bridges, and five more traps.

6 power moves 12 extra bridges 5 extra traps 3 drills + a quiz
1 Start here

You already have the bridge. Now you need the tie-breakers.

Almost nobody misses an analogy because they couldn't think of a relationship. They miss it because two choices both fit the relationship they thought of, and they picked by feel.

Every technique on this page is a way of breaking that tie with a rule instead of a hunch. Read the moves, then use the drills — a technique you've only read about disappears the moment the clock starts.

The one idea behind all six moves: a bridge is not just "how these words are connected." A bridge has a direction, a word form, a strength, and it is either always true or only sometimes true. Two choices can share the connection and still differ on all four. That's where the points live.
If you haven't yet Read The Verbal Method Lab first — it covers building the bridge, the ten most common relationships, and the five classic traps. This page assumes all of that.
2 The core

Six power moves

Learn these in order. The first three cost about one second each and will win you more points than any amount of extra vocabulary.

🏹 Move 1 · Draw the arrow

Don't just say the bridge — give it a direction you can see. Write the two words with an arrow and the verb sitting on the arrow. Direction stops being something you have to remember and becomes something you can look at.

hammer —drives→ nail

Now every choice has to run the same way. wrench —tightens→ bolt ✅ · nail —is driven by→ hammer ❌ same relationship, flipped.

Why this matters more than it sounds: the backwards choice is the single most common wrong answer on the whole section, because it is genuinely the right relationship. It only ever gets caught by keeping the order physical.

🪞 Move 2 · The word-form mirror

Before you think about meaning at all, look at what kind of words the stem uses. Verb : verb needs a verb : verb answer. Adjective : noun needs adjective : noun.

beg : borrow → both VERBS → the answer must be a VERB

So on beg is to borrow as offer is to ___, the choices lender, security and bank are all nouns and all dead before you read them. That leaves repay and lend — a two-way choice instead of a five-way one.

Cheapest point on the test This takes one second and typically deletes two or three choices. Do it on every analogy, even the ones you think are easy — especially those, because that's where you're moving fast enough to be careless.

💯 Move 3 · The "always true?" test

This is the strongest tie-breaker there is. A real bridge is true by definition — not most of the time, not usually, but always. Say your sentence and put the word always in it.

"A cygnet is always a young swan." ✅ That's a definition.
"A judge is always formal." ❌ Often, sure. Always? No — that's a description, not a definition.

When two choices survive your bridge, the one that is necessarily true beats the one that is usually true, every time. The test writer builds the tempting wrong answer out of a characteristic that's merely common.

🔄 Move 4 · The reversibility check

A well-built bridge still makes sense when you read it backwards. If flipping it produces nonsense, you've described the pair loosely rather than defined it.

"A chisel carves marble" ↔ "Marble is carved by a chisel." ✅ Both true.
"A poem relates to a poet" ↔ "A poet relates to a poem." ❌ Both are vague, which means the bridge was never real.

Use this when you're stuck: if you can't reverse your sentence cleanly, rebuild it with a stronger verb.

🧭 Move 5 · The double bridge (for A : B :: C : ? questions)

When the question gives you three words and asks for the fourth, there are two bridges available, and you get to pick whichever is easier.

Across (the normal one): snake —is the category of→ python, so dog —is the category of→ terrier.
Down (the backup): snake and dog are both general animals; python and the answer should both be specific breeds.

Build the across bridge first. Then, if two choices survive, run the down bridge as a second, independent test. A choice has to pass both.

Careful The down bridge is a cross-check, never the main tool. Plenty of wrong answers are built to look right going down and fail going across.

🔍 Move 6 · The specificity ladder

Your bridge should be exactly as specific as the stem is — no looser, no tighter.

Too loose and four choices survive. Too tight and none do. So treat specificity as a dial you turn, not a thing you get right first time:

🔽 Four choices survive? Add one detail. Not "opposites" but "opposites about seriousness." Not "smaller unit" but "a tiny metric unit next to a much larger non-metric one."
🔼 No choice survives? You built in a detail the test doesn't care about. Strip one word out and run it again.

The rule: never choose between two survivors by feel. Turning the dial takes three seconds and converts a coin flip into a certainty.

All six moves on one hard question

MILLILITER is to QUART as ___

  1. Arrow: milliliter —is far smaller than→ quart
  2. Word-form mirror: noun : noun. Every choice is noun : noun, so nothing dies here. Fine — move on quickly.
  3. First bridge (loose): "a smaller unit next to a bigger unit."
(A) pint is to quart  — survives
(B) foot is to yard  — survives
(C) millimeter is to yard  — survives
(D) pound is to gram — backwards: big to small
(E) inch is to yard  — survives
  1. Four survivors → turn the dial up. What is actually special about milliliter and quart? Two things: the gap is enormous (about a thousand times), and they come from two different systems — metric and non-metric.
  2. Tightened bridge: "a tiny metric unit next to a much larger non-metric one, about a thousand times apart."
(A) pint is to quart — same system, only 2×
(B) foot is to yard — same system, only 3×
(C) millimeter is to yard — metric → non-metric, about 914× ✅
(D) pound is to gram — backwards
(E) inch is to yard — same system, 36×

The lesson isn't the answer. It's that at step 3 you had four live choices and no way to choose — and the fix was never "look harder at the options." It was go back and improve the sentence.

3 Practice

Drills · make the moves automatic

💯 Drill A · Always true, or only sometimes?

Move 3. Read the sentence and decide whether it's a definition (always true) or just a description (often true). Definitions make bridges; descriptions make traps.

🏹 Drill B · Same direction, or flipped?

Move 1. The stem pair is on the left. Does the choice run the same way, or is it the right relationship backwards?

🪞 Drill C · Alive or dead on word form?

Move 2. Ignore meaning completely. Does the choice match the stem's parts of speech — or can you cross it out without thinking at all?

4 The rescue kit

What to do when you don't know a word

This will happen several times on every test, and it is not a reason to skip. You can often get to the right answer without ever learning what the word means.

🧩 Rescue 1 · Break the word into pieces

Most long words are built from parts you already know. Polymorphous = poly (many) + morph (shape) = many-shaped. Amorphous = a (without) + morph = shapeless.

PieceMeansWords it unlocks
bene- / mal-good / badbenefit, benevolent · malice, malfunction
mis- / mal-wrong, badlymisjudge, misplace, maladjusted
a- / an-withoutamorphous, atypical, anonymous, apathy
-ous / -fulfull ofjoyous, perilous, bountiful
-lesswithoutfearless, ruthless, feckless
-ist / -er / -ora person whobotanist, lender, sculptor
-ology / -ologiststudy of / one who studiesgeology, pathology, sociologist
-ate / -ify / -izeto make or dofacilitate, clarify, sanitize
poly- / mono-many / onepolygon, polyglot · monotone, monopoly
re- / de-again / away fromrebuild, revive · deflate, demolish

A shortcut inside the shortcut: the ending alone tells you the part of speech even when the root means nothing to you. -ist and -or are people. -ate and -ify are verbs. -ous is an adjective. That's Move 2 for free.

⚡ Rescue 2 · Is it a good word or a bad word?

You often know a word's charge — positive or negative — even when you can't define it. Malevolent feels bad. Benevolent feels good. That's usually enough.

Then ask which way the stem's charge runs, and require the same of the answer:

fundamental (+) —→ frivolous (−) so the answer must run + —→ −

On that question, truth (+) → nonsense (−) matches. Fantasy → fiction doesn't move at all, and regulation → rule doesn't either. Charge alone gets you there.

🔙 Rescue 3 · Work backwards from the choices

If the stem is a total mystery, flip the whole process. Build a bridge for each choice instead, and then ask which of those bridges could possibly be true of the stem.

Choices that have no clear bridge of their own can be crossed off immediately — the test never makes the answer a pair with a weak relationship. Often three of the five are junk pairs, and you're guessing between two.

Bonus pattern If four choices share a relationship type and one is different, the odd one out is very often the answer — the test writer built four decoys to one shape.

🗣️ Rescue 4 · Where have I heard this word?

Don't try to define it. Try to use it. Put it into any phrase you've actually heard, and the meaning usually arrives with the phrase.

Impede → "a speech impediment" → something in the way. Facilitate → "a facility" → makes something easy. Arid → "an arid desert" → dry. Plaintiff → "a complaint" → the one complaining, so the one suing.

And the math on guessing A wrong answer costs ¼ of a point. A blank costs a whole point of opportunity. So the moment you can cross off even one choice, guessing is worth more than skipping. The rescue kit almost always gets you at least one cross-off — which means you should almost never leave an analogy blank.
5 The library, extended

Twelve more bridges

The Method Lab has the ten most common ones. These twelve show up constantly too — and several of them are the exact bridges that get missed, because they look like bridges you already know until you check them properly. Tap each card.

How to actually use the library Don't memorize twenty-two names. The point is that when you look at a pair, your brain should offer you a shortlist instead of a blank page. If nothing on the list fits within about five seconds, the pair is probably synonyms, antonyms, or degree — those three cover roughly a third of every test.
6 Know the enemy, part two

Five more traps

The Method Lab covers the topic trap, the backwards pair, the sound-alike, the opposite and the second meaning. Here are five more, each with the move that kills it.

Trap 6 · The almost-synonym

A word that goes with the stem word rather than means it. Settle and remain feel connected — settle down, stay put — but they are not interchangeable.

Fix · the swap test. Put both words into the same real sentence. "They settled the argument" / "They remained the argument." If the swap breaks, they aren't synonyms, no matter how related they feel.

Trap 7 · The right idea in the wrong word form

The choice has the correct meaning attached to the wrong part of speech: lender where the answer is lend, fanatic where the stem wants an adjective.

Fix · Move 2, the word-form mirror. Check the stem's parts of speech before you read any choice. This trap only ever catches people who read for meaning first.

Trap 8 · Sometimes-true instead of always-true

A pair whose relationship is real but optional. Pensive : seer — a seer might be thoughtful. Wealthy : doctor — often, not always. It reads as perfectly sensible, and it's wrong.

Fix · Move 3. Insert the word always and see whether the sentence survives. Definitions beat descriptions.

Trap 9 · The reversed degree

Intensity questions have a direction, and the trap runs it backwards. If the stem goes mild → strong (warm : scorching), then drenched : damp is strong → mild — the right relationship pointing the wrong way.

Fix · Move 1. Draw the arrow, and put the bigger word on the arrowhead: warm —grows into→ scorching. Now a flipped choice looks obviously wrong instead of vaguely right.

Trap 10 · The bridge that's the wrong size

The choice is related, but at a different level of zoom. Stem: chapter : novel (a named division of a work). Choice: word : sentence — technically part & whole, but a far smaller, more granular relationship. Or the reverse: a choice so specific that it adds a condition the stem never had.

Fix · Move 6, the specificity ladder. Ask whether your sentence describes the choice at the same zoom level as the stem. Same relationship at the wrong scale is still wrong.
7 Under the clock

The fifteen-second routine

All of this has to fit into the time you actually have. Here it is as one ladder — climb it in order, and stop as soon as one choice is left standing.

1 secCover the choices. Look only at the stem pair. Reading the options first is what installs the wrong bridge in your head.
1 secNote the word forms. Verb:verb? Adjective:noun? Now you know what shape the answer has to be.
4 secSay the arrow sentence. Two words, one strong verb, one direction. If you can't build it, jump straight to the rescue kit.
5 secRead every choice into the sentence, in order. All five, even the ones you doubt. Cross out physically as you go.
3 secOne survivor? Circle it and go. Two or more? Turn the dial: add a detail, or apply the always? test. None? Loosen once.
1 secStill stuck? Guess from what's left and move on. One cross-off makes guessing profitable. Never leave it blank, never spend a third pass on it.
The discipline that's hardest to keep When you're unsure, the instinct is to re-read the five choices hoping one starts to look better. It never does — re-reading choices just makes the most familiar-sounding one feel right. When you're stuck, go back to the sentence, not to the options.

📓 Turning misses into points

A practice test teaches you nothing by itself. The learning is entirely in what you do with the misses. Every wrong analogy has exactly one of these six causes — write the number next to it in your log:

  1. Didn't know a word → goes on the vocabulary list. The only cause that isn't free to fix.
  2. Bridge too loose → you never turned the dial. Practise Move 6.
  3. Backwards → you didn't draw the arrow. Move 1.
  4. Wrong word form → you skipped the mirror. Move 2.
  5. Sometimes-true → you didn't ask always? Move 3.
  6. Same topic, different relationship → you trusted the subject matter instead of the sentence.

Five of the six are method. If most of your log says 2–6, your score can move a long way in a couple of weeks without learning a single new word. If most of it says 1, the answer is the vocabulary trainer, not more analogies.

Ten minutes a day beats one test a week: twenty analogies, arrow sentence said out loud on every single one — including the easy ones, because that's where the habit gets built cheaply. Then once a week, redo only your misses from the papers you've already done.
