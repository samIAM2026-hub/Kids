1  Why out loud

Your eyes can skim. Your mouth can't.

A poem is built for the ear. Silently, you can slide over a line in half a second and never notice that it was doing something clever. Out loud, you have to decide where to breathe, where to slow down, and which word gets the weight — and those decisions are the poem.

You are also writing a poem this month for the BASA contest. Reading a great one daily for a few weeks is the cheapest training there is: by October your ear will already know how a good poem moves, and you'll hear it when your own lines go flat.

2  Four moves

How to read a poem so it sounds like a poem

Move 1 — Breathe at the punctuation, not at the end of every line.
Beginners stop at every line break, and the poem comes out choppy, like a shopping list. A line break is a small lean, not a full stop. In this poem lots of sentences run across two, three, four lines before they finish. Follow the periods and commas with your breath; lean on the line breaks with your voice.
Try: find the very first sentence on your sheet and read it in one breath, all the way to its period, even though it crosses several lines. Then do the same with the next one.
Move 2 — Short line, slow line.
When a poet drops a line that is only two or three words long, they are pulling the handbrake. Those lines want air around them. Give them a beat before and a beat after.
Try: circle the five shortest lines on your sheet. Read the poem again and put a real silence — count one — after each of those five.
Move 3 — Let the volume change with the poem.
The poem starts big and loud (enormous animals, hills, forests) and then goes small and quiet (silence, breath, whispering). If you read the whole thing at the same volume, you flatten it. Start it full-voiced. Let the middle drop to almost nothing.
Try: read the last stanza at the volume you'd use to tell someone a secret. Notice that the poem still works — it may even hit harder.
Move 4 — Repeated openings are a drumbeat. Keep the beat steady.
Several stanzas start with almost the same words. That's on purpose — repetition is how a poem builds pressure, like a drum under a song. Say each repeat with the same rhythm and the same weight, so the listener's ear catches the pattern.
Try: highlight every stanza opening that repeats. Read only those lines, one after another, and listen to the pattern by itself.
3  Stanza by stanza

Walk through it once with your finger on the page

Tap a stanza to see what to listen for. Everything below refers to your printed sheet — the stanzas are in the same order there.

The poem opens with enormous things reacting: hills, lions, elephants. Notice that Angelou never says "this is sad." She shows you heavy animals moving, and you feel the size of the loss without being told. That's the whole trick of the poem — big feeling, shown through concrete things.
Reading it: full voice, unhurried. These are heavy creatures; don't let them trot. Let the verbs land.
Now the camera zooms from elephants down to the smallest creatures in the forest, and the sound goes out of the world. Watch how fast the scale changes — huge, then tiny. Poets do this on purpose: switching scale makes a reader feel unsteady, which is exactly what grief feels like.
Reading it: drop your volume the moment the stanza starts. You are following the poem down into the quiet.
This is the hinge of the poem. The trees were never only trees — the stanza turns and says so directly. Then it describes something very hard to describe (what the air feels like when someone dies) with three plain adjectives, and it lands on memory: the walks never taken, the kind words nobody said. Those small regrets are more devastating than any big statement would be.
Reading it: slow right down on the three adjectives, one at a time, with a small gap between them. Don't rush the list.
Short stanza, and it's about the people still alive. The word wizened is worth looking up if you don't know it — it means shrivelled and dried out, the way a fruit goes when it loses all its water. Angelou is saying the survivors themselves get smaller.
Reading it: this one is nearly all short lines. Handbrake. Let each line sit.
Here the poem says the grief isn't madness — it's ignorance, being left in the dark and cold with the light gone. Notice how the earlier word radiance and the later dark, cold caves are set against each other. One image answers the other. That's called a contrast, and it's doing the emotional work of an entire paragraph in four words.
Reading it: the last three words of this stanza sit alone on their own lines. Say them like three separate sentences.
The poem does not end in the cave. Peace comes back — slowly, and not on a schedule. The senses come back, but changed. Then the ending goes to very short sentences and repeats itself, and the repetition is the point: it sounds like someone steadying their own breathing. A poem that ends by turning toward something better, after going all the way down first, is far stronger than one that was cheerful the whole time. Remember this when you write your own.
Reading it: quiet, slow, and stop fully between those last short sentences. Then don't say anything for three seconds. That silence is part of the poem.
