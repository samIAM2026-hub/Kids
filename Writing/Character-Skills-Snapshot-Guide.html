01 · Start here

What is the Snapshot?

The Character Skills Snapshot helps schools understand how you see yourself: how you learn, handle challenges, and work with other people. It is for students applying to enter grades 6–12 and usually takes about 25 minutes. [1]

You are still growing.

Your answers describe how you see yourself now. Schools do not expect you to be equally strong in every area. You can keep building these skills as you grow. [2]

Your job: read carefully, understand the instructions, and answer honestly. The official guidance says you do not need special test preparation. [2]

The reading tips and made-up examples below are suggestions for understanding the format. They are not official questions or a scoring guide.

02 · About-you questions

“Most like me” and “least like me”

You will see three statements. Choose the one that is most like you and the one that is least like you. [2]

Made-up example · No answer key
  • I enjoy working through a tricky problem.
  • I adjust easily when a plan changes.
  • I enjoy completing a task with a group.

How to think about your answer

  1. Picture your usual self. Think about school, home, sports, clubs, or time with friends.
  2. Recall a real example. When did you last act this way? Does it happen often or only occasionally?
  3. Compare the three statements. Which fits you most closely? Which fits you least closely?

“All three sound like me!”

Ask which one feels most natural or happens most often. Then choose the one that fits you least closely out of these three.

“None sounds exactly like me!”

Choose the closest match. You do not need a statement that describes you perfectly.

Remember: “least like me” means the least similar of these three statements. It does not mean you never show that quality.

Answer about how you usually act now, rather than how you hope to act someday. Avoid using one unusually good or difficult day to describe your whole self.

03 · Situation questions

“How appropriate is this response?”

You will read a situation and several possible responses. Rate each response on the scale shown, from not appropriate to very appropriate. This is different from choosing just one best response. [2]

1. Read the facts

What happened? Who is involved? Is there a deadline, a commitment, or a safety issue? Use the information the question gives you.

2. Consider the effects

What might happen next? How could this action affect the people involved and the task they need to finish?

3. Judge this response

How suitable is this particular action in this situation? Follow the scale and instructions on the screen.

4. Move to the next one

Read the next response on its own. Do not assume you must use every rating exactly once.

Made-up example · For discussion before the test

You and two classmates are making a poster due tomorrow. One classmate has not sent their part, and you do not know why.

Imagine a response: “Send a message asking whether they need help and when they can send their work.”

Think: What information could this give you? How might it affect the teammate? What would still need to happen before the deadline?

Open a thinking prompt

Explain your reasoning in your own words. Then consider how your judgment might change if the deadline were five minutes away. The details of a situation matter; there is no rating supplied here to memorize.

A useful distinction: an about-you question asks what describes you. A situation question asks how appropriate an action is. Read the question before deciding which kind of thinking it needs.

The official guidance encourages honest answers and says there are no right or wrong answers. Its scoring explanation also says situation judgments are compared with ratings from teachers and admission staff. Give each situation careful thought. [2] [3]

04 · Helpful habits

Small habits that make questions clearer

  • Read the whole sentence. Notice words such as “most,” “least,” “usually,” and “appropriate.”
  • Think of a specific moment. A real memory can help you decide whether a statement fits you.
  • Use the same honest view of yourself. You do not need to remember previous answers or build a perfect image.
  • Avoid fixed formulas. “Always ask an adult” or “always do everything yourself” will not fit every situation. Consider the facts.
  • Take a calm breath if you feel stuck. Reread the instructions, make your best honest judgment, and continue.

Before the assessment: you can discuss these examples with a parent. During the assessment, make your own choices and follow the official rules shown in your account.

05 · Useful words

Understand the language

Most / Least

Most: the closest match.
Least: the weakest match among the choices.

Appropriate

Suitable for this particular situation. Ask whether the action makes sense in the circumstances.

The seven skills, in everyday language

Intellectual engagement
Being interested in learning and exploring ideas.
Open-mindedness
Being willing to try something new.
Initiative
Getting started on a task in good time.
Resilience
Recovering and adjusting when things change or go wrong.
Self-control
Managing what you think, do, and say.
Social awareness
Understanding situations involving other people and responding suitably.
Teamwork
Supporting others and working together.

Plain-language summaries of the official skill descriptions. [1]
