Three Pieces, One Performer

A funny one, a serious one, and one with six voices.

BASA · rehearsal coach
Keep your three marked sheets next to you. This page doesn't retype them — the words belong to their authors and they live on your paper, along with your teacher's pencil marks. This page is the coach: it explains the marks, shows you how to deliver each part, and helps you get all three into your memory.
1  The job

Three pieces that need three different performers

Your three sheets were chosen to be as different from each other as possible. That is the point: the skill being trained is range — changing your face, your speed and your voice so completely that each piece feels like a different person is on stage.

🦸My PowersDemetri Martin
Comic monologue. One character, dead serious, completely ridiculous. You move around the stage.
🌳When Great Trees FallMaya Angelou
A poem about losing someone great. Still body, slow voice, real silence.
⚡Zeus Kills EveryoneRick Riordan
Storytelling. A narrator plus five characters, and you are all of them.

The map is drawn the way the audience sees you, so "stage left" is on the right of the picture. Stand up, face an imaginary audience and point: your left hand is stage left.

Why "up" and "down"? Old theatre stages were built on a slope, higher at the back so everyone could see. Walking away from the audience really was walking uphill.
3  Piece by piece

How to deliver each one

Tap a piece to open it. The numbered beats follow your sheet from top to bottom — one beat is one paragraph (or one stanza).

What it is. A man warns you about his super powers. He believes every word. The joke is that almost every "power" falls apart halfway through the sentence — it turns out to be useless, ordinary, or actually a weakness.

The engine: brag, then flop. Nearly every paragraph is built the same way. First half: a big superhero boast. Second half: the boast collapses. Your whole job is to make the first half sound 100% impressive so the collapse is a surprise.

Three rules of deadpan

1. Never smile. "Deadpan" means a completely serious face while saying something silly. The character has no idea he is funny. The second you grin, you are telling the audience "this is a joke" — and it stops being one.

2. Tiny pause before the flop. Find the turn in each paragraph — the word where the brag starts to go wrong — and put a half-second of air right before it. That gap is where the laugh loads.

3. Wait for the laugh. If people laugh, stand still and let them. Start the next line as the laugh is dying down, not on top of it. If nobody laughs, carry on just as proud as before.

Your four crosses, and why they are there

A good cross isn't decoration — it does something for the line. Look at what each of yours does:

  • XL at the three dots in the "secret" line. The walk is the pause. You are making them wait for a secret you will never tell.
  • XR as the "versatile" paragraph starts. New spot, new topic — and a versatile hero should look like he can pop up anywhere.
  • XUS on the running paragraph. You walk to the back, so when he tells them how far away he is now, it is actually true. The move is the punchline.
  • XDS on the last paragraph. Come all the way down to the audience for the final warning. Closest you have been all piece — then hold still on the last four words.
Walk first or talk first? Try each cross both ways — moving in silence and then speaking, or speaking as you go. For the XL, silence is funnier. For the XUS, try walking while he brags, and arrive just in time for the "over here" line.

Beat map — 18 paragraphs

  1. The warning. Calm, low, a little dangerous. You are doing them a favour by telling them.
  2. Seeing the future. First flop of the piece. Say the ending as if it were the impressive part.
  3. Breath, then hands. Two jokes back to back. Look at your own hands with respect.
  4. Strength (the ice cream). Longest sentence so far — take your time and enjoy the patience at the end.
  5. Fire. One quick line. Proud of something that is obviously bad news.
  6. Lasers. The flop is boring on purpose. Sound mildly inconvenienced.
  7. XLThe secret. Cross on the dots. Smug.
  8. Wind, rain and sulking. Builds in three steps. The last step should sound like a mighty threat.
  9. Board games. "Fierce" is the key word. Then the flop: he is just a bad sport.
  10. The doctor. Speed up a little, like someone who really has asked the same thing five ways.
  11. The yo-yo. Short and dark. Toss it away casually.
  12. XRVersatile, sidekicks, broccoli. The broccoli is pure nonsense — say it with total confidence, then look straight at them for the little question that ends the paragraph.
  13. Weaknesses. He can't even list a weakness without bragging. Slow down for the lava.
  14. Helpful and honest. Sweetest voice of the piece… then the flop, flat.
  15. XUSRunning. Cross upstage. Lift your volume from back there — he tells them to.
  16. Poison. A flop with a comeback attached. Proud again by the end.
  17. Swimming and chips. Two quick ones. The quoted words are someone else's opinion — say them as a compliment.
  18. XDSThe bleeder. Cross down. Quiet and threatening. The last four words: a full stop after them, hold the stare, count two, then relax.

Words to be sure of

lethal — deadly; able to kill.
lest — "for fear that"; so that something bad doesn't happen. Old-fashioned, which is why it sounds grand.
flammable — catches fire easily.
sulk — to be silent and grumpy because you are annoyed.
fierce — intense, aggressive.
versatile VER-suh-tile / VER-suh-tl — able to do many different things.
sidekick — a hero's helper (Robin to Batman).
immune — can't be harmed by something.
ingest — to swallow; take into your body.
One thing to notice: "immune" and "ingest" are fancy science words, and he uses them to say "poison only hurts me if I swallow it" — which is true of everybody. Fancy word + obvious fact is one of the piece's favourite tricks. Say the fancy words slowly and clearly.

What it is. A poem about what happens to the people left behind when someone great dies. It starts with huge animals and falling trees, moves to people, goes down into a dark place, and climbs out at the end.

You have already worked on this poem — the stanza-by-stanza walk-through is on Reading a Poem Out Loud. What is new on this sheet is your teacher's marks.

Reading the slashes and arrows

Every slash is a real stop. Count them on your sheet — they sit at the end of each complete thought, not at the end of each line. Between two slashes, the words are one thought and get one breath. This is the "breathe at the punctuation" move, now marked for you.

Two arrows. One sits after the lions' line in the first stanza, and one in the fourth stanza after the words about our reality. At both, the line breaks in the middle of a phrase. If you stop there, the sentence makes no sense for a second. Lean on the last word and roll straight on.

Two slashes in the middle of a line. In the last stanza, a slash cuts one line in half, and another does it again in the final line. Stop exactly there, even though your eye wants to finish the line. The second one sets up the very last sentence of the poem — give it room.

Try: read the poem tapping the table once at every slash and sliding your finger along at every arrow. Taps = silence. Slide = no silence. When that feels easy, do it without your hands.

The opposite of Piece 1

In My Powers you walk, you stare people down, you play a character. Here you do none of that. Plant your feet. No crosses, no acting the animals. Your face is calm and your eyes go out over the audience. All the movement is in your voice: full at the start, sinking through the middle, nearly a whisper in the caves, then warmer and steadier as peace comes back.

The ending. The last few sentences are very short and they repeat. Say each one as its own sentence with a full stop after it. After the final word, stay still and silent for three seconds before you move. That silence belongs to the poem.

What it is. A storm giant from the pit leaves home to destroy the gods. The gods take one look at him and panic. Zeus is left alone — and gets insulted into fighting.

The engine: giant monsters, ordinary talk. The events are enormous (smashed cities, boiling seas) but everyone speaks like a regular family on a regular day. A world-ending monster talks to his wife like a dad leaving for work. The king of the gods is scared and trying not to show it. Play the ordinary talk as really ordinary, and the contrast does the comedy for you.

Your cast — one voice each

The colours below are how the highlighting looks on the scan of your sheet. Check them against the paper, then write a three-word voice plan for each character: pitch (high / low), speed (fast / slow), attitude. The grey text is only a starting idea.

Narrator — no highlight. Your own voice, talking straight to the audience. Dry and amused, like you know how silly this all is.
Typhoeus — blue. The storm giant.
Echidna — purple. His wife. One speech, and it is a complaint.
Poseidon — pale blue. Two tiny lines.
Athena — pink. The clever one.
Zeus — orange. Loud on the outside, shaking on the inside.
Make the voices far apart. The test: someone listening with their eyes closed should know who is speaking before the narrator tells them. If two characters sound alike, change one thing about one of them — usually speed is the easiest.

Three storyteller tricks

1. Give each character a place to look. Narrator: straight at the audience. Typhoeus looks down (everyone is smaller than him). The gods look up. Zeus looks sideways at the other gods when he is rallying them, and up when he yells at the giant. A small turn of the head is enough — snap to the spot, speak, snap back to the audience for the narrator.

2. Read the punctuation out loud. Capital letters mean volume. Italics mean lean on that word. And when the gods shout their battle cry the second time, the sheet prints it with a question mark — so your voice has to go up at the end, like you are not sure this is a good idea.

3. The narrator's side comments are jokes too. A few times the narrator stops the action to point something out — whether a threat is logical, or why Zeus cares about ants. Drop the story voice for those, lean in a little, and say them like a quick aside to a friend.

Beat map — 12 beats

  1. Restless in the pit. Narrator. Start casual — this is how you would tell it at lunch.
  2. Leaving for work. Typhoeus, then Echidna, then Typhoeus. He is cheerful; she has had this argument before.
  3. Smashing the upper world. Narrator. Let the list of destruction build, but keep it light — for him this is easy.
  4. The challenge. Typhoeus, shouting into the distance. Bored and disappointed.
  5. The gods see him. Narrator, then Poseidon, then Athena. Pause before the narrator's "until". Poseidon gulps; Athena stays precise.
  6. Zeus's pep talk. Zeus, all confidence. Then the narrator quietly tells the truth about how he really feels.
  7. The charge. Zeus gives the order. The gods follow — and their battle cry comes out as a question.
  8. The giant's battle cry. Typhoeus at full volume, then the narrator's aside about why the threat doesn't quite make sense.
  9. Full height. Narrator. The one truly scary paragraph. Slow down and let it be big — the comedy needs one real threat.
  10. The gods run. Three panicked cries, each smaller and sillier than the last. Then one flat narrator line.
  11. Zeus alone. Zeus is furious at the others. Typhoeus laughs and mocks him — slow, huge, delighted.
  12. The ant. Narrator's aside, completely reasonable tone. Then Zeus's last shout, and the final sentence quick and clean. Stop. Don't trail off.

4  Memory

Build your own cue card

You don't memorise a piece word by word from the top. You memorise the order of the beats, and hang the words on that. For each beat, look at your sheet and type one to three words that will bring the whole beat back to you — the first words, or the funniest word. Your own cue works much better than one somebody hands you.

Then press Test me: the descriptions disappear and only your cues are left. Turn your sheet face down and perform from the cue card.

The ladder. Step 1: perform with the sheet in your hand. Step 2: sheet face down, cue card showing. Step 3: cue card hidden too — close this page and go. Climb one step only when the step below is easy. If one beat keeps falling out, perform just that beat and the one before it, five times, joined together.
5  Every day

Fifteen minutes, on your feet

Rehearse standing up, out loud, at full performance volume — your memory stores the piece together with the movement and the voice, so mumbling it in a chair is practising a different piece. The boxes clear themselves each morning.
