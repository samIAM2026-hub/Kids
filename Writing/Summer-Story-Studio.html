Read this first — 30 seconds

  1. You write it. Nobody else. Your parents ask questions and hand you photos, that's all. A clumsy sentence you wrote beats a polished one an adult wrote — at the interview, the person across the table will ask you about this essay, and it has to sound like you.
  2. Adults ask "why". Each story ends with three layers of why. Interviews usually walk down those same three layers, so say the answers out loud before you need them.
  3. Photos are clues, not homework. Open your own camera roll while you answer Step 1. You're looking for details — how hard the wind was blowing, how dirty your hands got, who laughed first.

Step 1 Scroll the camera roll

Open your summer photos — camping, the trips, camp. Find six that pass the test below, and for each one write a single line: what is happening in this photo? Nothing gets uploaded here; you're just writing down what you see.

Which photos to pick

  • Photos where you are doing something — hands busy, body moving: pitching a tent, lighting a fire, paddling, patching a tire
  • Photos where something went wrong — broken gear, rain, a mess, a face falling apart
  • Photos with a real expression — tired, focused, laughing, completely zoned out
  • Before and after — half-built versus finished
  • Pure scenery and sunsets. Beautiful, but you're not in them
  • Posed group shots where everyone smiles at the camera
  • Anything so far away that nobody can tell what is going on

Step 1b Your YouTube shorts

The clips you cut for your teacher. Paste each link (set them to Unlisted when you upload, so only people with the link can watch), then answer: which 3 seconds matter most, and why did you keep them?

Step 2 Story hunt

Eight questions to shake stories loose. One line each, and skip anything that brings nothing to mind. The point isn't to write well here — it's to get every candidate out of your head so you can see them side by side.

Five boxes Fill these before you write a sentence

One or two sentences each, and fragments are completely fine. Once all five are full, the essay is about 80% done. Box 3, the one detail, is the most valuable sentence in the whole piece.

What counts as a detail

A detail is something your five senses can grab onto. Not "it was hard" — "my fingers were too cold to tie the knot." Not "the tent broke" — "the snapped end kept poking through the fabric."

The trick: close your eyes, go back to that exact minute, and ask what did I hear? what was in my hands? what did it smell like? Whatever you answer goes straight into Box 3.

Peek at somebody else's example

This is somebody else's story. Borrow the shape, never the sentences — your details will look nothing like theirs, and that is exactly what makes your essay worth reading.

The draft Put it together in your own voice

Connect the boxes in the order below and you have a paragraph. Use the words you actually say. If you wouldn't use a word at the dinner table, leave it out — readers can always tell which sentences an adult wrote.

  1. Open with a moment, not a summary. Don't start with This summer I learned a lot about myself. Admissions readers see hundreds of those. Drop straight into the scene: The tent pole snapped at two in the morning.
  2. Middle: problem → detail → what you did. Keep the part where your first fix didn't work. "I solved it right away" reads as made up, and the second attempt is the interesting one anyway.
  3. Ending: two or three sentences on how you felt and what you think now. No slogans — Never give up! tells the reader nothing. Write the small true thought: I still came in last, and I was fine with that.
