01 What this is being marked against

An admissions reader gets a few hundred of these in a season and most get about two minutes each. They are not marking your English — that is what the SSAT is for. They are asking one question: can I picture this kid in our classroom? Five things decide it.

02 What you handed in

The task: tell one true story about something you did where something went wrong or turned out harder than you expected, and what you did about it.
Handed in: 20 September 2026 · 391 words · 3 paragraphs, but the middle one is 330 words on its own.
In this story I'll be explaining how I overcame my fear of public speaking and how it affected me as a person. Before I had public speaking class, I would have some opportunities in school to speak in public such as assemblies, or presentations. During these sessions I would feel terrified which caused me to stutter, miss words, and lose track of where I was on the page. Because of this, no one wanted to be my partner for group projects involving speaking. When I told my mom about this she signed me up for a public speaking class without even telling me as she knew that I would not approve. On the first day of class, everyone had a speech and was reciting them out loud. They even used hand gestures which made them look extremely professional. When it was my turn, I didn't prepare a speech as it was my first class. Therefore, my teacher gave me the topic “talk about a time when someone else saved you from doing something humiliating” and told me to improvise. I got on that stage, hands shaking and just stood there for what felt like 5 minutes without saying a word. Eventually the teacher just told me to come down and said that he needed to talk to my parents about an assessment of my level. I walked down from that stage probably looking as red as a tomato while everyone was staring at me looking annoyed. The next week when I came back, I got to know the process of how they did things there. First the teacher would give you a topic and you would have to write a speech about it, then you would memorise the speech and say it in front of the class. At first I would just get on stage and not say a word and I would be told to get off but slowly I improved and started actually talking until I could get on stage and was just about as good as everyone else. Ultimately the process was quite terrifying. However, now when I speak in front of the class I don't stutter at all and people actually want me to be their partner now! This affected me tremendously as it caused me to get higher marks on presentations due to new found confidence.

1 · Fix You announced the essay

A reader can see what the essay is about by reading it. Telling them first is like explaining a joke before you tell it — and it uses up the one sentence that decides whether they read carefully or skim.

Draft 2: delete this sentence completely. Open on the stage, in the silence. Do not replace it with a different summary.

2 · Good Fear shown as facts

You didn't write “I was very nervous.” You wrote what the fear actually did — stutter, miss words, lose your place on the page. Three specific things. That is exactly the move the rest of the essay needs more of.

3 · Fix Big claim, no evidence

“No one wanted to be my partner” is the saddest line in the essay and you give it seven words. A reader can't feel it because you didn't put them in the room.

Draft 2: one moment instead of the summary — the teacher says “find a partner,” and what happened in the next ten seconds. What did you look at? Who ended up with you, and how?

4 · Good Only you could have written this

Your mum signing you up without even telling me as she knew that I would not approve is true, slightly funny, and tells the reader something about your family in one line. Keep it word for word.

5 · Fix Lab-report word

Therefore belongs in a science write-up, not in your own voice. You wouldn't say it out loud.

Draft 2: “So my teacher gave me a topic instead…”

6 · Good The best detail in the whole piece

Being handed talk about a time when someone else saved you from doing something humiliating — and then being humiliated on stage, with nobody saving you — is a gift. It is ironic, it is true, and no other applicant has it. Quote it exactly again in draft 2.

7 · Fix Your opening is buried here

This is where the essay should start. hands shaking is good — but the whole freeze gets one sentence, and the reader is through it before they can feel it.

Draft 2: give this four or five sentences of its own. What did you see from up there? What could you hear? What were your hands doing? How long before someone coughed? Slow it right down. (Also: write five minutes, not 5 minutes.)

8 · Fix Say it the way it happened

an assessment of my level is office language. The real version is harder and better: in front of everyone, the teacher decided you might be in the wrong class.

Draft 2: “…and said he needed to talk to my parents about whether I was in the right class.”

9 · Fix Borrowed phrase

as red as a tomato is a phrase you've read, not something you noticed. And probably tells the reader you're guessing about your own story.

Draft 2: what did it actually feel like from the inside? Ears hot, throat dry, the walk back to your seat being far too long — pick the one that is true.

10 · Fix You can't know this

You couldn't see whether they were annoyed — you were the one on stage. Guessing at other people's feelings makes a reader trust you less, and it makes the class sound mean when the real problem was inside you.

Draft 2: report only what you could actually see or hear. Nobody laughing is often worse than laughing — if that's what happened, say that.

11 · Fix This is a timetable, not a story

Three lines explaining how the class ran — topic, write it, memorise it, say it. The reader doesn't need the system; they need what it was like to be you inside it. This is also where you slip into you would have to write a speech, which stops being about you at all.

Draft 2: cut it to one clause, or cut it entirely. Stay in “I”.

12 · Fix ★ The biggest problem — you skipped the part that matters

Weeks of change, the entire point of the essay, in one sentence: slowly I improved and started actually talking. The reader wants to know how, and right now the honest answer the essay gives is “time passed.”

This is also where the ownership goes missing. Your mum signed you up, the teacher set the topics, and improvement just… happened. Nowhere do we see you decide something.

Draft 2: this becomes your longest paragraph. Pick one specific class. What was the topic that day? What did you do differently that week — where did you practise, how many times, did you say it to a wall, to your sister, in the shower? Name the first sentence you ever got all the way through. One real day beats “slowly I improved” every time.

13 · Fix Hedging word

quite terrifying — quite makes it smaller. Either it was terrifying or it wasn't. And by now the reader already knows it was, so the sentence isn't earning its place.

14 · Fix The exclamation mark is doing the work

An exclamation mark is the writer telling the reader to be impressed. Take it away and the sentence has to prove itself — which is the point.

Draft 2: one fact instead: who asked you first, and when. “Last month Ethan asked me before the teacher finished the sentence.”

15 · Fix Your ending settles for marks

You had the whole essay to earn a real thought, and the thought is “I get higher marks now.” Every applicant wants higher marks — it tells the reader nothing about you. affected me tremendously and due to are also the two stiffest phrases in the piece, right where the writing should sound most like you.

Draft 2: two or three honest sentences about what you do now that you would not have done before. Volunteering to go first. Sitting with the kid who has gone quiet. Something you can point at. No slogan, no “this taught me,” no exclamation mark.

06 The three things that decide the next draft

  1. Show the turn, don't report itOne specific day, one specific speech, and what you did that week. Longest paragraph in the essay. If a reader can't say what you did differently, the essay hasn't happened yet.
  2. Start inside the moment, end with a real thoughtCut the announcement at the top; the stage is your first line. At the bottom, cut the marks; end with one thing you do now that you didn't do before.
  3. Five paragraphsFrozen on stage · how you got there · the weeks of silence · the day it changed · now. A 330-word block tells a reader you handed in the first thing you wrote.

Same story. Don't go looking for a better one — this one is good, it just isn't written yet. 300–350 words, five paragraphs. Write it from the start; don't patch draft 1, because patching keeps the old shape.

Paragraph 1 · about 70 words

Your first line is the teacher handing you the topic, or the silence. No explaining, no “in this story”. Hands, chair, the class waiting, your mouth opening. Quote the topic you were given word for word.

Paragraph 3 · about 50 words

Paragraph 4 · about 100 words · ★ the one that matters
The day something changed — and what you did
One class. What was the topic? What did you do differently that week, and where? How many times did you run it? What was the first sentence you got all the way through, and what happened when you did? This is the paragraph a reader remembers or forgets you by.
Paragraph 5 · about 50 words
Now
Two or three sentences. One concrete thing you do now that you couldn't do before — not a mark, not a slogan, no exclamation mark. Then stop. Don't add “and that is how I overcame my fear.”

09 One more thing — the interview

Whoever reads this may interview you, and they will ask about it. “You said you practised — where?” “What was the first speech you actually finished?” “Who was the kid nobody would partner with in your class — did you notice them?”

That is another reason draft 1 is risky: there is almost nothing in it to ask about, and the parts that could be asked about (“slowly I improved”) you'd have to invent on the spot. Write the true details down and the interview answers are already yours.

10 Overall comment

Warren — the hard part of an application essay is finding something real to write about, and you already have it. A boy who is genuinely afraid, a mother who signs him up anyway, a teacher who hands him “talk about a time when someone else saved you from doing something humiliating” and then watches him stand there in silence. That is a better story than most people your age manage to find, and the honesty in it does you credit. You didn't tidy yourself up, and that matters.

What holds it at a 3 is that you reported your story instead of writing it. The essay tells us at the top what it is about, runs 330 words without a paragraph break, and then covers months of change — the only part a reader is actually waiting for — in a single clause: “slowly I improved.” The ending reaches for marks when it should reach for a thought. And the way it currently reads, the two people who changed things are your mum and your teacher; you are the one it happened to.

None of that is a talent problem. Every fix on this page is a decision about where to spend your words, and you already proved you can do it — “stutter, miss words, and lose track of where I was on the page” is exactly the right kind of writing, in the third sentence of the piece. Do that to the day it changed, and this becomes an essay a reader remembers at the end of a long afternoon.
