01 What that sheet was asking you to do

The instruction at the top of the page
“Then test it: cover the target word and read the sentence aloud. Can the meaning still be guessed? If not, the clue is too weak — fix it now.”

That is a long sentence. Let me say it in a shorter way.

What it really means Write a sentence using the hard word. Then hide the hard word with your thumb and read the sentence out loud. If somebody who has never seen that word could still guess what it means — your sentence is good. If they could not guess — your sentence needs more help in it.

The word “target word” just means the hard word — immaculate, dubious, haggle, contemplated. That is the word you are hiding.

You wrote four sentences. Every one of them uses the word correctly. That part is good. But none of them would pass the thumb test. Try it below and you will feel why.

02 Try the thumb test yourself

Press the button. The word turns into a black block. Then read the sentence out loud and try to guess what the missing word means.

Your sentence
My room is immaculate.
Could you guess it? No. The missing word could be messy, small, blue, cold, warm — anything at all. Nothing in the sentence points to clean. So this sentence has no clue in it.
The same sentence, with a clue added
My room is immaculate — there is not one sock on the floor, and every book is straight on the shelf.
Now you can guess it. No socks on the floor. Books all straight. The missing word must mean very clean and tidy. That is what a clue does — it points at the meaning without saying it.

That is the whole lesson on this sheet. The extra words after the dash are the clue. Everything below is just doing the same repair to your other three sentences.

03 Your four sentences, one at a time

For each one I check three things: is the word used correctly, is the spelling and grammar right, and does it pass the thumb test.

Sentence 1 · immaculate
My room is immaculate
✓Word used correctly? Yes. A room really can be immaculate. Good choice.
✓Spelling and grammar? All correct. Well done — that is a hard word to spell.
✗Passes the thumb test? No. Cover the word and it could be any word at all.
Fixed — the clue is after the dash My room is immaculate — there is not one sock on the floor, and every book is straight on the shelf.
Sentence 2 · contemplated
I bot a new hat and my mom wa contemplated.
✗Word used correctly? Not quite. You contemplate something — there is always a thing after it. “My mom was contemplated” does not work. She has to contemplate the hat.
✗Spelling? bot should be bought. And wa should just be cut out.
✗Passes the thumb test? No clue at all.
The bit to remember Contemplate = look at something and think hard about it for a long time. You always need to say what she was thinking about.
✗ She contemplated.
✓ She contemplated the hat.
Fixed I bought a new hat, and my mom contemplated it for a long minute, turning it around and thinking hard before she said anything.
Sentence 3 · haggling
My mom and dad was haggling about a braslet
✓Word used correctly? Yes — and this is your best sentence of the four. Two people arguing about the price of something is exactly what haggling is.
✗Grammar? “mom and dad” is two people, so it is were, not was. Count the people first: one person → was, two people → were.
✗Spelling? braslet → bracelet. Two little words stuck together: brace + let.
✗Passes the thumb test? Still no. Cover “haggling” and it could be talking, arguing, reading, dreaming.
Fixed — the clue is the back-and-forth numbers My mom and dad were haggling over a bracelet, going back and forth — twenty dollars, no, fifteen, no, eighteen — until they finally shook hands.
Sentence 4 · dubious
I was dubious
✓Word used correctly? Yes. “I was dubious” is real English and it is right.
✓Spelling? Perfect.
✗Passes the thumb test? No — and here the problem is length. Four words is not enough room to hide a clue in. A clue needs somewhere to live.
Fixed — say what made you doubt it He said he had cleaned his whole room in two minutes, and I was dubious — I raised one eyebrow and did not believe a single word.

04 Three easy ways to build a clue

You do not have to invent something clever. Pick one of these three and use it every time.

Way 1 · the dash
Put a dash — then describe it.

“The bike was immaculate — not a speck of rust, not a scratch.”
This is the one the story itself uses. Copy it.

Way 2 · because
Add the word “because” and explain.

“I was dubious because nobody cleans a whole room in two minutes.”

Way 3 · an example
Show it happening.

“They were haggling — twenty dollars, no, fifteen, no, eighteen.”

Then always finish with the thumb test. Cover the word. Read it out loud to Mom or Dad. Can they guess? If yes, you are done.

06 The homework at the bottom of the sheet

Copied from your page — and there is a quiz on it
“Collect 3 unknown words from anything you read this week. Make a clue card for each: copy the whole sentence, circle the clue, write your guess, then check a dictionary and tick or correct it. Warm-up quiz next lesson.”

That last line matters — your teacher is going to test you on these next lesson. So this one is not optional.

A “clue card” has four parts, in this order:

  1. Copy the whole sentence the word came in. The whole sentence — not just the word on its own.
  2. Circle the clue inside that sentence. The clue is the other words that helped you. Not the hard word.
  3. Write your guess at the meaning. Guess before you look it up. Always. That is the part that trains your brain.
  4. Check a dictionary. Then tick your guess if you got it, or write the correct meaning if you did not.
Where to find your three words Any book you are reading. A page of a novel. A sign. Even the back of a cereal box. Pick words you truly do not know. Picking easy ones wastes the homework — and the quiz will use the words you picked.

08 One thing before you go

Grace — look at what you actually got right on this sheet. You used all four hard words correctly. Immaculate, contemplated, haggling, dubious. You knew what they meant and you put them in real sentences. That is the hard part, and you did it on your own.

What was missing was not understanding. It was just the last step — adding the clue. And a clue is only a few extra words: a dash, a because, or an example.

So next time you write a sentence with a hard word in it, do not stop at the full stop. Cover the word with your thumb, read it out loud, and ask: could somebody guess? If not, add five more words. That is all it takes.
