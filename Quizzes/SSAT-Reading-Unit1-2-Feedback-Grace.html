03 The two you missed — and why

Unit 2 · Question 1
Why is Dev sitting behind the portable classrooms?
You put(a) He is in trouble with a teacher.
Answer(b) He does not want to talk to anyone.
The proof is in his own words. Dev says: “I didn't want to sit in there and have somebody ask me how my weekend was.” That is the answer. He is hiding from questions, not from a teacher.
The trap: you saw the word “trouble”. Priya says “You're going to get in trouble out here.” Going to means it hasn't happened yet. The story used the word, so option (a) felt familiar — but familiar is not the same as correct. Test makers do this on purpose.
The habit to build: before you circle an answer, point at the line in the story that proves it. If you can't point at a line, it's the wrong answer.
Unit 2 · Question 2
What can you infer about Dev's mother?
You put(a) She is a good cook.
Answer(c) She feels guilty about news she has not told him.
“Infer” means the story does NOT say it. You have to work it out. So any answer that is just sitting there on the page — like “she cooks his favourite dinner” — is too easy to be an inference. The story never says the food is good. It says it is his favourite, and that she made it three nights in a row, including noodles on a night that isn't a noodle night.
Put the three clues together:
1. A form for a school in Calgary, with Dev's name filled in — in pen.
2. “Did she tell you?” → “Not yet.”
3. Suddenly, favourite dinners three nights running.
A mum who has already decided something big, hasn't said it yet, and is being extra nice → she feels guilty. That's (c).
Same mistake as Question 1. Both times you picked the answer that used words you had just read. Both times the real answer needed you to add two clues together. That's the one thing to change.

04 Your short answers, fixed

You had good instincts in three of these. What's missing is evidence from the story — a short quote or a specific action. An SSAT short answer is basically: my idea + the proof.

Unit 1 · Question 4 · left blank

Unit 2 · Question 4 · half marks
Why does Priya put the sandwich on the backpack instead of handing it to him?

Your instinct is right — Priya is being careful. But “scared” is a bit strong, and it puts the focus on Priya's feelings when the real point is Dev's. 05 Your paragraph — what to change

Unit 2 · Writing · What kind of friend is Priya?
Priga is a kind freand and shose kindness, so when Dev is sad she calms him down, and say nice words.

What works

You started with a claim — “Priya is a kind friend” — which is exactly step 1. That's the right shape.

What's missing

  1. No evidence from the story“She calms him down and says nice words” never happens. Priya barely says anything — and when she does try (“Maybe it's just a form”) Dev cuts her off. Her kindness is in what she does: she sits down on the cold step, she gives him half her sandwich, she stays after the bell. Use one of those.
  2. No explanationStep 3 is “This shows ___ because ___”. You need to say why the action proves your claim.
  3. No closing sentenceStep 4: say the claim again in different words. One short sentence is enough.
  4. Only one sentenceThe task asked for 3–4 sentences. Four steps = four sentences. If you write one sentence per step you can't get it wrong.
Model paragraph · all four steps

Priya is a quietly loyal friend. In the story, she finds Dev behind the portables, sits down on the cold concrete step beside him, and puts half her sandwich on top of his backpack without saying anything. This shows she knows what Dev needs, because he doesn't want to be asked questions — so instead of talking she just stays and shares her lunch. Priya is the kind of friend who does not need words to help.

Spelling & grammar in this paragraph: Priga → Priya · freand → friend · shose → shows · “she say” → she says.

06 Two instructions you read backwards

Instruction 1 · while reading
“Circle the words that gave you the clue — not the hard word itself.”

On the story page you underlined immaculate, dubious, haggle and contemplated — the hard words. Those are the ones you're solving. What you mark are the words that help you: for immaculate → “not a speck of rust, not a scratch, oiled and gleaming”. For dubious → “she frowned, tilted her head, not at all convinced”. Same colour pencil, other words.

Instruction 2 · the writing task
“Build a clue into the sentence so someone who never read the story could work the word out.”

You wrote four sentences (the task only asked for two — nice effort!), but none of them has a clue inside. Cover the target word and read your sentence aloud: could a stranger guess it? Right now, no. Here's each one repaired.

Your sentenceProblemWith a clue built in
My room is immaculate. True sentence, but no clue. Cover “immaculate” and it could be any word — messy, small, blue. My room is immaculate — not one sock on the floor, and every book straight on the shelf.
I bot a new hat and my mom wa contemplated. “bot” → bought. And contemplated needs a thing after it — you contemplate something. “was contemplated” doesn't work. I bought a new hat, and my mom contemplated it for a long minute, turning it around and thinking hard before she said anything.
My mom and dad was haggling about a braslet. Best of the four — the word is used correctly. “mom and dad” = two people → were. braslet → bracelet. Still no clue. My mom and dad were haggling over a bracelet, going back and forth — twenty dollars, no, fifteen, no, eighteen — until they finally shook hands.
I was dubious. Correct use of the word. But three words is not a sentence with a clue in it. He said he had cleaned his room in two minutes, and I was dubious — I raised one eyebrow and did not believe a single word.

The test for a good clue sentence: cover the hard word with your thumb, read the sentence to someone who has never seen it, and ask them to guess. If they can't, add a “because”, a “—”, or an example.

09 Three rules for next time

Rule 1
Point at the proof before you circle.

Put your finger on the line in the story that makes your answer true. No line, no circle. This one habit would have fixed both of your wrong answers.

Rule 2
“Infer” means it is NOT written down.

If an option just repeats something the story already said out loud, it's the trap. The real answer is the one you had to build from two or more clues.

Rule 3
Every short answer = idea + proof.

Never write only the idea. Add “In the story, …” and one specific thing that happened. Two sentences beats one every time. And never leave a line blank — a guess can earn marks; a blank can't.

11 One last thing

Grace — a nine-year-old who can work out what dubious means from a frown and a tilted head is a nine-year-old who can read. That part is already working.

What tripped you up isn't reading. It's slowing down at the last step: you find the right idea in your head, then write down the first version of it instead of the best one. “She was scared he didn't want it.” “They want to keep talking.” Both times, your first thought — and both times the story had a better answer waiting two lines away.

So here's the whole trick, and it takes ten seconds: after you write your answer, go back and find the line that proves it. If you can't find one, change your answer. That's it. That's the difference between 4 out of 6 and 6 out of 6.
