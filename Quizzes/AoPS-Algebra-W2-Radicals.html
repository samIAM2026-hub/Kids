√ Roots and Fractional Exponents

A root is just an exponent in disguise: the square root is the power ½, the cube root is the power ⅓. Once you see that, every Week 1 rule works on roots too — and messy-looking problems turn into small, friendly numbers.

Where you are on AoPS

Week 2 on AoPS has 12 problems on fractional exponents and radicals, and none of them are started yet. That’s fine — this page walks through every kind of problem in that set: roots as exponents, negative bases, multiplying and simplifying radicals, cube and fourth roots, the “smallest n” question and the fill-in-the-blank puzzles. Every one has a twin here, so when you open the real set it will already feel familiar.

1 · What a fractional exponent means

Why is 91/2 the same as √9? Use the Week 1 power-of-a-power rule: (91/2)2 = 91/2 · 2 = 91 = 9. So 91/2 is the number whose square is 9. That is exactly what √9 = 3 means. In the same way a1/3 is the cube root and a1/4 is the fourth root.

For an exponent like 2/3, read it in two parts: the bottom says which root, the top says which power.

82/3 = (3√8)2 = 22 = 4

You may do the root or the power first — the answer is the same. But root first keeps the numbers small.

Example
Compute 6253/4.
  1. Bottom is 4, so take the fourth root first: 5 · 5 · 5 · 5 = 625, so 4√625 = 5.
  2. Top is 3, so cube it: 53 = 125.
Answer: 125
Example
Compute 93/2 + 642/3.
  1. 93/2: square root first, √9 = 3, then cube: 33 = 27.
  2. 642/3: cube root first, 3√64 = 4, then square: 42 = 16.
  3. Add: 27 + 16 = 43.
Answer: 43
⚠️ Trap

6253/4 is not 625 × 3/4. An exponent is never a multiplier. And don’t cube 625 first — you would get 244,140,625 and then need its fourth root. Root first, power second.

Remember

Bottom of the fraction = which root. Top of the fraction = which power.

2 · Negative exponents and negative bases

A negative exponent still means flip, just like in Week 1. A fraction exponent still means root and power. So with something like a power of −2/3 there are three jobs: flip, root, power. Do them one at a time.

What about a negative base? An odd root of a negative number is fine: 3√−8 = −2, because (−2)3 = −8. An even root of a negative number has no real answer, because any number squared is 0 or more.

Example
Compute (−27)−2/3.
  1. Flip: (−27)−2/3 = 1 ÷ (−27)2/3.
  2. Cube root: 3√−27 = −3, because (−3)3 = −27.
  3. Square: (−3)2 = 9. So the answer is 1 ÷ 9.
Answer: 19
Example
Compute (827)−4/3.
  1. Flip the fraction to get rid of the minus: (278)4/3.
  2. Cube root of top and bottom: 3√27 = 3 and 3√8 = 2, so you have (32)4.
  3. Fourth power: 34 = 81 and 24 = 16.
Answer: 8116
⚠️ Trap

The negative exponent does not make the answer negative — it only flips. And a negative base does not always give a negative answer: in (−27)−2/3 the −3 gets squared, so the answer is positive. Compare (−32)−3/5: the fifth root is −2, and (−2)3 = −8 is odd, so that answer is −1/8. Look at the top of the exponent to decide the sign.

3 · Multiplying square roots

You may multiply square roots into one big root: √a · √b = √ab. Why? Square the left side: (√a · √b)2 = a · b. So it is the square root of ab. Fractions work the same way.

The smart move is to not multiply everything out. Break each number into primes, cancel what you can, then look for pairs. Every pair of equal primes under a square root comes out as one copy.

Example · mental math
In your head: √285 · 457.
  1. Cancel across before multiplying: 28 and 7 give 28 ÷ 7 = 4. 45 and 5 give 45 ÷ 5 = 9.
  2. Now it is √4 · 9 = √36 = 6.
Answer: 6
Example
Simplify √2 · √10 · √15 · √21.
  1. Put it under one root and write primes: 2 · (2·5) · (3·5) · (3·7).
  2. Find pairs: two 2s, two 3s, two 5s. Each pair sends one copy outside: 2 · 3 · 5 = 30.
  3. Left alone inside: the 7.
Answer: 30√7
⚠️ Trap

Multiplying combines roots. Adding does not. √9 + √16 = 3 + 4 = 7, but √25 = 5. So √a + √b is not √a + b.

4 · Simplest form and adding like radicals

A square root is in simplest form when no perfect square is left hiding inside it. Pull out the biggest perfect square you can find: √72 = √36 · 2 = 6√2.

Once roots are simplified, you can add the ones with the same number inside, like counting apples: 3√2 + 5√2 = 8√2. But √2 and √3 are different fruit — they stay apart.

Example
Simplify √50 − √18 + √98.
  1. Each one hides a square times 2: √50 = √25 · 2 = 5√2, √18 = √9 · 2 = 3√2, √98 = √49 · 2 = 7√2.
  2. Now they are like radicals: 5 − 3 + 7 = 9.
Answer: 9√2
Example
Simplify √75 + √48 − √108.
  1. √75 = 5√3 (75 = 25 · 3), √48 = 4√3 (48 = 16 · 3), √108 = 6√3 (108 = 36 · 3).
  2. Combine: 5 + 4 − 6 = 3.
Answer: 3√3
⚠️ Trap

Two common slips. (1) √50 − √18 is not √32 — simplify each root first, then combine. (2) The root of the square comes out, not the square itself: √20 = 2√5, not 4√5.

Remember

Know your squares: 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144. They are what you look for inside a root.

5 · Cube roots, fourth roots, and “the smallest n”

Same idea, bigger groups. For a square root you need pairs. For a cube root you need groups of three. For a fourth root you need groups of four. Each full group comes out as one copy.

Big numbers ending in zeros have a shortcut: 1000 = 103, so 3√1000 = 10 can be peeled off first.

Example
Simplify 3√432000.
  1. Peel off the thousand: 3√432 · 1000 = 10 · 3√432.
  2. Look for a cube inside 432: 432 = 216 · 2 and 216 = 63.
  3. So 3√432 = 63√2, and 10 · 6 = 60.
Answer: 603√2
Example · smallest n
What is the smallest positive integer n so that 4√72 · n is an integer?
  1. Primes: 72 = 23 · 32.
  2. For a fourth root every exponent must be a multiple of 4. The 2s need one more (3 → 4). The 3s need two more (2 → 4).
  3. So n = 2 · 32 = 18. Check: 72 · 18 = 1296 = 64 ✓
Answer: n = 18
⚠️ Trap

Don’t stop at a perfect square. With n = 2 you get 144 = 122 — nice, but 4√144 is not a whole number. A fourth root needs groups of four. In the same way, a pair like 9 = 3 · 3 is not enough to escape a cube root.

6 · Same base with fractions, and letters under roots

Some problems multiply three ugly pieces, like a fraction to the power ⅓ times another to the power ⅔. Each piece alone is a messy decimal. Together they are friendly. The plan is Week 1’s plan: write every base as primes, multiply out the exponents, then add each prime’s exponents separately.

Letters work the same way. Turn every root into an exponent: √x = x1/2, 3√x2 = x2/3. Then the fill-in-the-blank puzzles become a small equation for the box.

Example
Compute (274)1/3 · (12)2/3 · 161/3.
  1. Primes: 274 = 33 · 2−2, 12 = 2−1, 16 = 24.
  2. Multiply exponents: 31 · 2−2/3, then 2−2/3, then 24/3.
  3. Follow the 2s: −2/3 − 2/3 + 4/3 = 0. They vanish! Follow the 3s: just 31.
Answer: 3
Example · fill in the blank
What number goes in the box? 3√x▢ · √x = x11/6
  1. Call the box b. Roots to exponents: xb/3 · x1/2.
  2. Multiply means add: b/3 + 1/2 = 11/6.
  3. Subtract: b/3 = 11/6 − 3/6 = 8/6 = 4/3, so b = 4.
Answer: 4
⚠️ Trap

The root number goes on the bottom of the exponent. 3√x4 = x4/3, not x3/4.

Remember

Every root is an exponent in disguise: the n-th root of xm is xm/n. Turn roots into exponents, and all the Week 1 rules work again.
