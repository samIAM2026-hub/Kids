Hi Grace! Eight flat shapes, and you have to say which of them would fold up into a cube. Folding in your head is hard, so this page gives you two rules that sort nearly all of them out without any folding at all.

Read the three sections, then take the test — 12 out of 15 (80%) to pass.

📦 A net is a solid unfolded

Cut a cardboard box along some of its edges and flatten it out. What you get is a net: every face laid side by side, still joined.

So the first check is simply counting. A cube has six faces, so a net of a cube must have exactly six squares — five will not close, seven will overlap.

🧠 Folding it in your head

Counting six squares is not enough — they have to be in the right places. Two tests catch nearly every wrong one:

1
No more than four in a straight line. Four squares in a row wrap right around the cube and meet. A fifth in the same line would land on top of the first.
2
The other two go one on each side of that row — the one above folds up to be the lid, the one below folds down to be the base. They can hang off any square of the row, even the same one. Two on the same side would both try to be the lid.

The family that always works: a row of four with one square attached above the row and one below, anywhere along it. The plus shape and the T shape both belong to this family. A few cube nets, like the zigzag staircase, have no row of four at all — they fold up too, but these two rules are for the row-of-four kind.

🧱 Rectangular prisms too

Question 2 asks the same thing for a rectangular prism. A prism has six rectangular faces in three matching pairs — front and back, left and right, top and bottom.

a net of a rectangular prism = 6 rectangles, in 3 matching pairs

So there is one extra check on top of the cube ones: the pieces that end up opposite each other must be the same size. A net with six rectangles that do not pair up cannot close into a box, however it is arranged.
