The 6 misses, explained

Each one starts with the sentence that should have been written first

37 · Impartial : disinterestedchose C · relevant : incoherentanswer: E · flammable : inflammable
The relationship: "Impartial and disinterested mean the same thing — both mean fair and not taking sides."
So the answer has to be another pair of words that mean the same thing. Look at the choices with that one sentence in hand: opportune : inopportune is opposites. relevant : incoherent isn't a pair at all — relevant means "on topic", incoherent means "doesn't make sense". Those two aren't opposites or synonyms; they're just two unrelated words.
The real point of this question is the trap. Flammable and inflammable look like opposites — and every instinct says in- means "not". But they are the same word: both mean "catches fire easily." (Fire trucks say INFLAMMABLE for exactly this reason, and it has caused real accidents.) The stem was doing the same trick: disinterested looks like it should mean "bored", but it actually means "having nothing to gain, so fair."
Warren's rule to write down: dis- and in- do not always mean "not." Disinterested = fair. Uninterested = bored. Inflammable = burns. Invaluable = priceless (super valuable!). Famous = well known. Infamous = well known for something bad.
42 · Polymorphous : shapechose E · celestial : planetanswer: A · variegated : color
The relationship: "Polymorphous means having many shapes."
Break the word into pieces — this is a word where the pieces hand you the answer: poly- = many (polygon = many sides, polyglot = many languages) and -morph = shape (metamorphosis = a change of shape). So polymorphous = many-shaped.
Now say it about the choices: variegated means having many colors — a variegated leaf is the one with green and white patches. Same sentence, perfect fit. Celestial : planet only means "a planet is in the sky" — nothing about many, so the sentence doesn't survive.
Bonus: choice B was hiding the same root — amorphous = a- (without) + -morph (shape) = shapeless. That exact word is the correct answer to question 57 further down. Learn the root once, win twice.
43 · Ardent : devoteechose C · pensive : seeranswer: D · formal : judge ✱
The relationship: "A devotee is always ardent — that's what makes him a devotee."
A devotee is a devoted fan or follower; ardent means burning with passion. The adjective isn't a random description — it's the defining quality. So the test is: does the adjective have to be true of that person?
A seer is someone who sees the future. Is a seer necessarily pensive (deep in thought)? No — that's just a mood you might imagine. A judge, though, is defined by formality: the robe, the courtroom, the procedure. That's the closest fit among the five.
Also check the word type. The stem is adjective : noun. Choice A (fanatic : zealot) is noun : noun, so it can be crossed out immediately even though the two words really are synonyms — that is the bait. Matching the parts of speech kills two or three choices on almost every analogy, for free, before any thinking.
45 · Insipid : foodchose D · doubtful : personanswer: C · banal : conversation
The relationship: "Insipid is the word you use to criticize food for being dull and flavorless."
Two things have to match, not one. First, the adjective has to be the kind of word that naturally goes with that noun. Second — and this is where the point was lost — it has to be the same kind of criticism.
Insipid food = no flavor, boring. Banal conversation = no originality, boring. Same complaint, different subject. That's an exact parallel. Doubtful : person is a real pairing, but "doubtful" means unsure — it has nothing to do with being dull, so the sentence doesn't carry across.
Choice B (sour : vinegar) is the other trap, and worth noticing: vinegar is supposed to be sour. That's praise, or at least just a fact. Insipid food is a complaint. Direction of feeling matters.
47 · January : Marchchose A · Monday : Augustanswer: E · Mars : Venus
The relationship: "January and March are two members of the same set — both are months."
When a stem gives two things from one category and nothing else connects them, the relationship is "same category." So the answer must be two members of one category too.
Mars and Venus are both planets. ✓   Monday and August are a day and a month — two different categories, which is exactly why it feels almost right and isn't. "Moon : night" is a thing and a time. "Winter : cold" is a season and a feeling. "Mercury : temperature" is a substance and what it measures. Only E has both feet in one category.
The hidden bonus link: January is named for Janus and March is named for Mars — both Roman gods. And Mars and Venus are both Roman gods. The test writer picked those two planets on purpose. (Note: the paper printed "march" in lowercase, which is a typo and makes the question look stranger than it is.)
57 · Balanced : unshapedchose E · external : internalanswer: C · symmetrical : amorphous
The relationship: "Balanced means evenly shaped; unshaped means having no form — they are opposites about form."
Choice E is opposites — that part was right, and that's why it was tempting. But external : internal is opposites about position (outside vs inside), not about form. When several choices are opposites, the winner is the one that is opposite along the same axis as the stem.
Symmetrical = perfectly balanced in shape. Amorphous = a- (without) + -morph (shape) = shapeless. Balanced form vs no form. Same axis, exact match — and it's that same -morph root from question 42.
Second layer of the sentence trick: don't just say "they're opposites." Say what they're opposite about. "Opposites about shape" instantly separates C from E.

The 4 that were left blank

All four are synonym or antonym stems — the easiest type. These were points sitting on the table.

34 · Exertion : endeavoranswer: C · defiance : opposition
Both words mean effort. Exertion = hard effort, endeavor = an attempt or effort. So find two words that mean the same thing: defiance and opposition both mean pushing back against something. Everything else on the list is an opposite pair (amateur/expert, feebleness/vigor, release/restraint, obedience/challenge) — four opposites and one synonym pair makes the odd one out very easy to spot.
38 · Arraign : indictanswer: B · accuse : charge
Both are courtroom words for formally accusing someone of a crime. Even without knowing arraign, the shape of the word (and indict next to it) says "legal."
This one needs a tie-breaker, because imprison : jail and plant : sow are also synonym pairs. When more than one choice has the right relationship, pick the one whose meaning also matches: accuse/charge is accusing, which is what arraign/indict mean. Imprison/jail is locking up (that happens later); plant/sow is farming (not legal at all). Relationship first, then meaning.
53 · Resolution : determinationanswer: C · integrity : honesty
Synonyms. Resolution (in the "New Year's resolution" sense — firmness of purpose) and determination both mean refusing to give up. So look for another pair meaning the same thing: integrity and honesty. Watch out for D (severity : compassion), which is opposites, and A and E, which are just loosely related.
55 · Facilitate : impedeanswer: E · delay : hasten
Opposites. Facilitate = make easier (think "facility"), impede = block or slow down (think "impediment"). Delay = slow down, hasten = speed up. Perfect opposites.
Choice C (sink : descend) is the trap — those are synonyms, and a synonym pair can never answer an antonym stem.
The math on blanks. A wrong guess costs ¼ of a point. A blank costs a full point of opportunity. Four blanks here were worth up to 4 points — more than double what all six wrong answers cost (1.5). Rule: if even one choice can be crossed out, guess. On all four of these, the relationship was synonyms or antonyms, which is the easiest type to sort — there was no reason to leave them.

Warren's method · say the sentence first

The red note at the top of the paper is exactly right. Here it is as four steps — the whole thing takes about ten seconds per question.

  1. Write the relationship as a full sentence. Not "they're related" — an actual sentence with a verb: "A cygnet is a baby swan." "Insipid is what you call food with no flavor." If the sentence can't be written, the words aren't understood yet, and guessing between two is better than staring.
  2. Say what kind of opposite or same. "Opposites about shape" beats "opposites." That single extra word is what separated C from E on #57, and it's the most common way points are lost.
  3. Match the word types. Adjective : noun stem needs an adjective : noun answer. This alone kills choice A on #43 and usually 2–3 choices on every question, before any real thinking.
  4. Break long words into pieces. poly- many · a-/an- without · -morph shape · -phile love · -cide kill · bene- good · mal- bad. Polymorphous and amorphous were both solvable from parts, with no dictionary.
  5. Never trust in-, dis-, or un- to mean "not." Inflammable burns. Invaluable is precious. Disinterested is fair. When a word looks like an easy opposite, that's usually the question's whole point.
  6. Guess whenever one choice is gone. Wrong costs ¼; blank costs the whole point.
Roots worth memorizing from this page:
  • -morph = shape → polymorphous (many shapes), amorphous (no shape), metamorphosis (change of shape)
  • poly- = many → polygon, polyglot, polymorphous
  • a- / an- = without → amorphous, atypical, anonymous
  • Words that came up and are worth knowing cold: insipid (flavorless), banal (unoriginal), ardent (passionate), arid (dry), exorbitant (way too expensive), erratic (unpredictable), interminable (endless), impede (block), facilitate (make easy), disinterested (fair), symmetrical (evenly balanced), pedestrian (ordinary), buffoon (a clown), cygnet (baby swan), gander (male goose), ewe (female sheep)
About the ✱ question. #43 (ardent : devotee) is a weak question. Formal : judge is the best fit among the five because it keeps the adjective-describes-the-person shape, but fanatic : zealot is defensible on meaning alone — those two really are synonyms, and a devotee really is close to a fanatic. It's only ruled out because it's noun : noun instead of adjective : noun. Don't lose sleep over this one; it's a flaw in the book, not in the thinking. The other 29 are clean.
