Hi Grace! Up to now, fractions have been about cutting one thing into pieces — one pizza, one cake. Exercise 12 does something new: it takes a whole group of things and calls the group "one whole".

Nothing gets cut up here. You just count. Read the four sections, then take the test — 12 out of 15 (80%) to pass.

🧺 The new idea — the whole is a group now

A set just means a group of things that belong together: 8 apples, 12 flowers, 20 beads. When we say "half of the set", the whole set is the 1, and half of it means half of all the things in it.

Here are 8 apples. Draw one line down the middle and you have 2 equal parts — 4 apples in each part. Each part is 12 of the set, even though a half is now 4 whole apples and not half an apple.

The one sentence to remember In a set fraction, the bottom number is how many equal parts the whole group is split into, and the top number is how many of those parts you are talking about. It is exactly the same rule as with a pizza — only now the "slices" are little piles of things.

1️⃣ Q1 — divide the set into equal parts

"Divide the set into 2 equal parts" means: draw lines so that every part has the same number of things in it. There must be nothing left over and no part bigger than another.

1
Count everything first. The pumpkins in your book: 8 in the top row and 8 in the bottom row, so 16 altogether. Always count before you draw a single line.
2
Divide. 16 ÷ 2 = 8. So each part must contain 8 pumpkins.
3
Draw the line so that 8 sit on one side and 8 on the other. Down the middle works; so does splitting it top row / bottom row. Any line is fine as long as the counts are equal.
4
Check by counting each part. 8 and 8. ✔

Part (b) is the same job with a different number: 18 popsicles into 3 equal parts. 18 ÷ 3 = 6, so you draw two lines and end up with three groups of 6.

⚠️ How many lines? To make 3 parts you draw 2 lines. To make 4 parts you draw 3 lines. It is always one fewer line than parts — like slicing a loaf of bread. Lots of people draw 3 lines for 3 parts and end up with 4 groups.

2️⃣ Q2 — what fraction of the set is shaded?

Now some of the things are coloured in and some are not. The question is: out of the whole group, how much is coloured?

1
Count the shaded ones → that is the top number.
2
Count everything, shaded and not shaded → that is the bottom number.
3
Simplify if the top and bottom share a number that divides into both.
⚠️ The single biggest mistake The bottom number is the whole set — that includes the shaded ones. If 6 flowers out of 9 are shaded, the answer is 69, not 63 (shaded compared with unshaded). Ask yourself: "how many are there altogether?" and put that number on the bottom, every single time.
And then simplify 69 is a correct answer, but 3 divides into both 6 and 9, so the tidy answer is 23. Both describe the same picture: 2 out of every 3 flowers are shaded.

3️⃣ Q3 — when the set is already split into groups

In Q3 your book draws lines through the sets, breaking them into equal groups. This is a gift — it means you can count groups instead of things, and the numbers stay small.

Look at the picture above. There are 4 equal groups and 3 of them are shaded, so the answer is 34.

Count it the slow way to prove it works: 9 shaded dots out of 12 altogether → 912, and 3 divides into both → 34. Same answer. Counting groups just skips the simplifying.

Before you count groups, check they are equal Counting groups only works if every group holds the same number of things. Count one group, count another, and make sure they match. If the lines make uneven groups, go back to counting things one by one.

4️⃣ Q4 — write a fraction in each box

Nothing is shaded here — instead the things are different kinds: green apples and red apples, circles and triangles and squares, black and white and grey beads. The question asks what fraction of the whole set each kind makes up.

The apples

There are 3 green apples and 9 red apples, so the set holds 12 apples altogether.

green: 3 out of 12 = 3/12 = 1/4   ·   red: 9 out of 12 = 9/12 = 3/4

Notice the two answers add up to 1 whole: 14 + 34 = 44 = 1. That is a free check you can do on every question of this type — if your fractions do not add up to the whole set, one of them is wrong.

The shapes and the beads

Same method, three kinds instead of two. Count each kind, count the total, write each one over the total, simplify. With the beads, watch out — the black beads come in two separate bunches at the two ends of the string. Add both bunches together before you write the top number; it is easy to count only the first group and forget the others.

⚠️ Part compared to part, or part compared to whole? "What fraction of the apples are green?" is asking part-compared-to-whole → 3 out of 12. If you write 3 out of 9 you have compared green with red, which answers a different question. The words "of the apples" tell you the whole set is the bottom number.
