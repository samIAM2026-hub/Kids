⚖️ Ratio and Percent

Percents and ratios look like easy school math, but AoPS hides traps in them. The big ideas: a percent is always “of” something, a ratio is a count of equal parts, and in a mixture you track the pure stuff — or whatever stays the same.

1 · Percent: change it, and undo it

“Up 25%” means multiply by 1.25. “Down 20%” means multiply by 0.80. Thinking of a percent change as one multiplication makes everything else easy.

new = old × (1 + rate)   so   old = new ÷ (1 + rate)

Percent change is always measured against the old number: change ÷ old × 100%.

Example
After a 25% raise, the rent on a flat is $1,500 a month. What was the rent before the raise?
  1. A 25% raise means the old rent was multiplied by 1.25: old × 1.25 = 1500.
  2. Undo the multiplication by dividing: old = 1500 ÷ 1.25 = 1200.
  3. Check: 25% of 1200 is 300, and 1200 + 300 = 1500 ✓
Answer: $1,200
Example · two changes in a row
A $50 game goes up 20%, then the new price goes down 25%. Overall, did it go up or down, and by what percent?
  1. Multiply both changes: 50 × 1.20 × 0.75.
  2. 1.20 × 0.75 = 0.90, so the final price is 90% of the start: 50 × 0.90 = 45.
  3. It went down 10% — not down 5% (25 − 20). Percents in a row multiply, they don’t add or subtract.
Answer: Down 10% (to $45)
⚠️ Trap

Taking 25% off the new number to go backwards. 25% off $1,500 is $1,125 — wrong, because the 25% was a percent of the old rent, not the new one. To undo “× 1.25”, divide by 1.25.

2 · Ratios are parts

A ratio like 3 : 5 says: cut everything into equal parts; one group gets 3 parts, the other gets 5. So there are 8 parts in total. Your first job is always to find how big one part is.

Read the words carefully. “3 out of every 8” is part-to-whole (the whole is 8 parts). “3 to 8” is part-to-part (the whole is 3 + 8 = 11 parts).

Example
At a summer camp, 3 out of every 8 kids are on the swim team. 150 kids are not on the swim team. How many are on it?
  1. “Out of every 8” means the whole is 8 parts. Swimmers are 3 parts, so non-swimmers are 5 parts.
  2. 5 parts = 150, so one part is 150 ÷ 5 = 30.
  3. Swimmers are 3 parts: 3 × 30 = 90.
Answer: 90
Example · four groups
A jar holds pennies, nickels, dimes and quarters in the ratio 2 : 3 : 4 : 1. There are 250 coins. How many dimes?
  1. Total parts: 2 + 3 + 4 + 1 = 10.
  2. One part: 250 ÷ 10 = 25.
  3. Dimes are 4 parts: 4 × 25 = 100.
Answer: 100
⚠️ Trap

In the camp problem, 150 is the kids who are not swimmers. If you divide 150 by 8 you are treating it as the whole — but 150 is only 5 of the 8 parts. Always ask: “How many parts does this number stand for?”

3 · When the ratio changes: bring in k

If boys to girls is 4 : 3, you don’t know the real numbers — but you know they are 4k and 3k for some whole number k. Write the before numbers with k, make the change, then use the new ratio to build one equation.

Example
In a club, boys to girls is 4 : 3. Then 2 boys leave and 5 girls join, and now there are exactly as many boys as girls. How many members does the club have now?
  1. Before: 4k boys and 3k girls.
  2. After: 4k − 2 boys and 3k + 5 girls.
  3. Equal now: 4k − 2 = 3k + 5, so k = 7.
  4. Boys 4·7 − 2 = 26, girls 3·7 + 5 = 26. Total 52. (Check: 26 : 26 is 1 : 1 ✓)
Answer: 52 members
Example · money swaps
Zoe and Max each have some money. If Max gives Zoe n dollars, Zoe has 5 times as much as Max. If instead Zoe gives Max n dollars, Zoe has 2 times as much as Max. Zoe’s money is how many times Max’s?
  1. Call their money Z and X. First swap: Z + n = 5(X − n), so Z = 5X − 6n.
  2. Second swap: Z − n = 2(X + n), so Z = 2X + 3n.
  3. Both equal Z: 5X − 6n = 2X + 3n, so 3X = 9n, X = 3n.
  4. Then Z = 2(3n) + 3n = 9n. Ratio 9n : 3n = 3 : 1. The n cancels — you never need it!
Answer: Zoe has 3 times as much as Max
⚠️ Trap

Don’t write the new numbers as 1k and 1k. The k belongs to the old ratio. Write the new numbers as “old k-expression ± change”, and let the new ratio give you the equation.

4 · Mixtures: track the pure stuff

You can’t add percents. 20% juice plus 50% juice is not 70% juice! What you can add is the amount of pure stuff. 300 mL of 20% juice holds 60 mL of pure juice. Write one equation that counts the pure stuff:

pure in A + pure in B = pure in the mix
Example
You have 300 mL of 20% juice. How much 70% juice must you add to get a 30% mix?
  1. Add x mL of 70% juice. Pure juice: 0.20·300 + 0.70x.
  2. The mix is 300 + x mL at 30%, so it holds 0.30(300 + x).
  3. Set equal: 60 + 0.7x = 90 + 0.3x, so 0.4x = 30, x = 75.
  4. Check: 60 + 52.5 = 112.5 mL pure in 375 mL, and 112.5 ÷ 375 = 0.30 ✓
Answer: 75 mL
Example · make a set amount
Using 70% juice and 20% juice, how much of each do you need to make 200 mL of 50% juice?
  1. Use x mL of 70%. Then the rest, 200 − x, is 20%.
  2. Pure stuff: 0.7x + 0.2(200 − x) = 0.5 · 200 = 100.
  3. 0.5x + 40 = 100, so x = 120.
  4. So 120 mL of 70% and 80 mL of 20%.
Answer: 120 mL of 70% and 80 mL of 20%
Remember

A mix is always between its ingredients. Mixing 20% and 70% can give 30% or 50% — but never 15% or 75%, because you can’t get stronger than the strongest thing you pour in. Bonus shortcut: the mix sits closer to the side you use more of. 30% is 10 away from 20% and 40 away from 70%, so you need 4 times as much of the 20% (300 mL vs 75 mL).

5 · Find what stays the same

When something dries, boils down or gets watered down, the percent changes — but one amount does not. Find that amount first. It is the key that unlocks the whole problem.

Example
A crate holds 80 kg of cucumbers that are 98% water. After a few days they are only 96% water. Only water was lost. How much do they weigh now?
  1. The dry part stays the same: 2% of 80 kg is 1.6 kg.
  2. Now water is 96%, so the dry part is 4% of the new weight: 0.04 × W = 1.6.
  3. W = 1.6 ÷ 0.04 = 40 kg.
  4. Surprise: the water percent fell by only 2 points, but the cucumbers lost half their weight!
Answer: 40 kg
Example · watering down
You have 40 L of salt water that is 15% salt. How much plain water should you add to make it 10% salt?
  1. The salt stays the same: 0.15 × 40 = 6 L.
  2. At 10%, 6 L of salt means the total is 6 ÷ 0.10 = 60 L.
  3. You had 40 L, so add 60 − 40 = 20 L of water.
Answer: 20 L
⚠️ Trap

Subtracting percents: “98% minus 96% is 2%, so it lost 2% of its weight.” That gives 78.4 kg — far from the truth. The percents are of different totals, so you can’t compare them directly. Work from the part that stays the same.

6 · Three equations, three unknowns

With three letters, the steady way is substitution in a chain: get one letter from the first equation, put it into the next, and so on. For example:

a − 3b = 1  ·  2b + c = 9  ·  a + 2c = 17

From the first two, a = 1 + 3b and c = 9 − 2b. Put both into the third: 1 + 3b + 18 − 4b = 17, so b = 2, then a = 7, c = 5.

But AoPS loves two faster tricks: add all the equations, and flip fractions upside down.

Example · add everything
At a snack stand, 2 fries and a burger cost $7.50. A burger and 2 shakes cost $6.50. One fries, 2 burgers and one shake cost $10.50. How much is one burger?
  1. Write f, b, s: 2f + b = 7.50, b + 2s = 6.50, f + 2b + s = 10.50.
  2. Add all three: 3f + 4b + 3s = 24.50.
  3. Now triple the last one: 3f + 6b + 3s = 31.50.
  4. Subtract: everything cancels except 2b = 7.00, so b = 3.50.
Answer: $3.50
Example · the flip trick
Find z if xyx + y = 5, xzx + z = 6, yzy + z = 10.
  1. Flip each equation upside down: x + yxy = 15.
  2. Split the left side: xxy + yxy = 1y + 1x. So 1x + 1y = 15, and the same way 1x + 1z = 16, 1y + 1z = 110.
  3. Add all three: each reciprocal appears twice, so 2(1x + 1y + 1z) = 1430, and the sum is 730.
  4. Take away 1x + 1y = 630: 1z = 130, so z = 30.
Answer: z = 30
Remember

When equations look messy but similar, try adding them all — the total often has a pattern. And when you see xyx + y, flip it: it turns into 1x + 1y, which is a plain, friendly equation.
