You asked how many to learn. Here is the honest answer, measured on your own papers. We took all 66 SSAT-level words we have real data on — the 30 synonyms from Practice Test 5 plus both drill rounds — and checked how many actually yield to word parts.

The finding that changes the plan. In those 66 words, 31 different roots unlocked only 26 words. Just two roots repeated at all. So at SSAT size, learning a root is close to a one-root-for-one-word trade — the famous promise that "one root unlocks dozens of words" is much weaker than people say. (Fair warning: 66 words is a small sample, and roots repeat more across the whole language than inside one test, so the payoff is real — just slower than advertised.)
But prefixes are a different deal, and this is the part worth your time. Prefixes are a closed set of under 30 that turn up again and again and again (in-, dis-, con-, pre-, re-, sub-, ex-, per-). Content roots are a long tail of hundreds, each showing up about once. And a prefix hands you the thing elimination actually needs: direction — not / against / before / away / under. So learn Tier 1 cold. Everything after that has falling returns.
So: how many? Tier 1 (29 prefixes) is the real investment. Tier 2 (37 roots) is worth it if you have the weeks. Tier 3 is for a rainy day. Stop after Tier 2 — that is 66 parts. Beyond that you are memorising a tail, and that time is better spent on the twins drill, where the payoff does not depend on knowing anything.

Tier 0 — free, nothing to memorise

Before any Latin: is there an ordinary English word hiding inside? This covers 18% of the words and costs nothing. You missed several of these on Test 5.

VIRTUOUS → virtue · you know what a virtue is
ORIGINATE → origin · where it began
RARITY → rare · hardly ever
MISERLY → miser · the gold-hoarder
TRIFLING → trifle · a tiny amount
STEADFAST → steady + fast · fixed firmly
DISJOINT → dis + joint · come apart
INCOMPARABLE → in + compare · cannot be matched
REASSURE → re + assure · make sure again
FASTEN → fast · as in "stuck fast"
The habit: before deciding a word is unknown, cover the ending (-ous, -ity, -ate, -ly, -ing) and look at what is left. Half the time an English word you have known for years is standing there.

The one prefix trap you must know. in- and im- mean not (invisible, impossible) but also into (include, import, inject). Two opposite jobs, same spelling. So in- tells you a word is probably negative, but never bet the whole question on it — check the choices before you commit.

Endings — not for meaning, for word type

These do not tell you what a word means. They tell you what kind of word it is — which is how you match pairs on analogies.

EndingMakes it aExamples
-ous, -ful, -ive, -able, -ible, -icdescribing wordgenerous, tenacious, credible
-tion, -sion, -ity, -ness, -ment, -orthing / ideabrevity, animosity, fortitude
-ise, -ize, -ate, -ify, -enactionscrutinize, placate, fasten
-lesswithoutflawless, reckless, hopeless
Why this matters on analogies. Remember Round 1, question 59: lumpy is to smooth. You picked heroic is to coward — but heroic is a describing word and coward is a thing. The shapes did not match, so the pair could not be right. Tumultuous is to still was two describing words, like the question. Check the endings before you commit.

How to actually learn them

  1. Tier 1 first, five prefixes a day. Twenty-nine prefixes is six days. This is the highest-value week of vocabulary work available to you, because prefixes repeat forever.
  2. Always learn through the anchor word, never the bare part. "greg = flock" will not stick. "A congregation is a flock of people, so gregarious means liking the flock" will. The anchor is a word you already own.
  3. Test yourself with the eye button at the top of this page — it blurs the meanings so you have to produce them, which is worth several times more than re-reading.
  4. Then Tier 2, five roots a day. That is another eight days. Stop there unless the test is still far off — 66 parts is the sensible ceiling.
  5. Keep doing the twins drill anyway. Parts fail on 42% of words. Twins never needs you to know anything, so it stays the first move — parts is the second.
The honest expectation. Learning all 66 parts will not turn every unknown word into a known one. On the evidence from your own papers it moves you from guessing among 5 to guessing among 2 or 3 far more often — and that is where the points are. Do not expect certainty; expect better odds, which is what the whole method is built on.
