🧰 Synonym toolkit — six moves, in this order

  1. Say your own meaning first. Cover the choices. “Deter = put off.” Then look for that.If you read the choices first, the traps choose for you.
  2. Put it in a phrase you have heard. “Expedited shipping”, “business acumen”, “the tide recedes”, “ratify a treaty”.Most hard words live in one or two common phrases. The phrase remembers the meaning for you.
  3. Break it into parts. re- back · de- away/down · ex- out · un- not · pre- before · ten hold · ced go · ped foot.recede = go back → retreat. deplete = un-fill → exhaust.
  4. Same part of speech. An action word needs an action word; a describing word needs a describing word.UTILIZE ≠ utilities · FRUGAL ≠ poverty · REPUTABLE ≠ fame. All three are noun traps.
  5. Spot the opposite. The test almost always puts the opposite in the list. If you see it, you know you have the right idea — the answer is its partner.TENACIOUS: timid ↔ unyielding · PERTINENT: irrelevant ↔ applicable · EXPEDITE: hinder ↔ hasten.
  6. Close is not enough — pick the exact one. When two choices feel right, ask which one matches every part of the meaning.corroborate = confirm (with proof), not just agree · deplete = use up, not just use.

Three of them (Q56, Q59, Q60) are one mistake: the “full of / lacking” direction. Learn that one check and those points come back.

🧰 Analogy toolkit

  1. Build the bridge before you look. Turn the first pair into one sentence: “A lecture is an event held in an auditorium.” Then drop each choice into the same sentence.
  2. Make the bridge tight. “Related to” and “part of” are too loose — two choices will survive. Add the key word: event, facial expression, bad habit, extreme.
  3. Check the direction: full of or lacking? Ravenous = full of hunger. Spurious = lacking authenticity. The test puts the reversed pair in the choices on purpose.This one trick would have saved Q56, Q59 and Q60.
  4. Match the type of word. Event with event (lecture/concert), person with person (liar/toady), feeling with feeling.
  5. Don't know a word in a choice? Test the others. If four choices fail the bridge, the one with the strange word wins — even if you have never seen it (toady!).

🧰 Reading toolkit — one method per question type

  1. Word in context (“most nearly means”). Cover the word. Read its sentence plus one before and one after. Put your own word in the gap. Then find the choice that matches yours.The clue is often a few lines away: “enfranchised” in line 17 explains “franchise” in line 27.
  2. EXCEPT / NOT questions. Turn it into a checklist. For each choice, find the line that proves it and write ✓. The choice you cannot prove is the answer. Never answer an EXCEPT question from memory.
  3. Watch absolute words. only, always, never, all, none — one counter-example makes the statement false.
  4. “Probably” / “most likely” questions. Use the passage’s own reasoning on the new case. Don’t bring in an idea the passage never mentioned, even if it sounds scientific.
  5. Tone of a named part. Read only the lines the question names. A poem can change mood at the end — the tone of the ending is the trap for a question about the beginning.

🎯 Four habits for the next mock

  1. Your meaning first, then the choices. On every synonym, say a meaning in your head before you read A–E. It takes one second and it switches off most traps.
  2. Never pick a look-alike. DOGMATIC → canine, PRETEXT → preface, RECEDE → requite, UTILIZE → utilities. If the only reason you like a choice is that it looks like the word, that is the trap.
  3. Bridge + direction on analogies. Say the bridge sentence out loud, and check “full of” or “lacking” before you pick.
  4. Prove it with a line. On reading, every answer — especially EXCEPT questions — should have a line number you could point to.
