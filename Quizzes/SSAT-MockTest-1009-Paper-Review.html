Teacher's comments

Warren, there is a lot to like here. You missed only one synonym out of the 28 you answered, and you got every question right on the subway story and the kite poem, with just one miss each on the Brooklyn Bridge and cello passages. The points you lost come from three things you can fix.

  1. Don't leave a whole passage behind. You skipped all six questions on the honeybee passage (Q7–Q12). Every one of them can be answered from a single sentence, and Q8 and Q9 are almost word-for-word in paragraph 3. If a passage looks hard, do its "According to the passage" and vocabulary questions first. They are quick points.
  2. Watch your time in Verbal, and know when to guess. In analogies 46–60 you answered 6 and left 9 blank. The blank ones were not hard: fuel is to engine as food is to body, anchor is to ship as root is to tree. Each wrong answer costs ¼ point and a blank costs nothing, so your rule stays the same: cross out at least one choice, then guess; if you can't cross out anything, skip it. On almost all of these you could cross out two or three.
  3. In Reading, answer the question that was asked. Two of your six misses picked something that is really in the passage but answers a different question. Q31 asked for the strongest reason and you chose the first reason. Q37 asked which finding shows crows learn from one another and you chose the finding about memory. Circle the key word in the question before you look at the choices.
  4. Say the bridge sentence for every analogy. Make one sentence that links the first pair, then test each choice with the same sentence. "A cub is a baby lion" → "A calf is a baby cow." "Hunger is fixed by food" → "Fatigue is fixed by rest." When two choices seem to fit, make the sentence more exact.

Words to learn from this test: plea, revere, sparse, fatigue, beacon, oasis, epidemic, brittle, elastic, pessimist, atlas.

Section 2 · Reading

Passage 2 · The honeybee dance (all six left blank)

Q7
You: blankCorrect: E explain how bees communicate the location of food

The whole passage follows one idea: a scout bee can't draw a map, so it dances, and the dance tells the other bees where the flowers are. Paragraph 1 sets up the problem, paragraphs 2 and 3 explain the dance, and paragraph 4 shows the other bees using it.

Von Frisch is in the passage, but only as the person who discovered the dance. It is not the story of his life, so D is too narrow.

Q8
You: blankCorrect: B the direction of the food relative to the sun

Paragraph 3 says it directly: "Its direction relative to straight up on the comb indicates the direction of the food relative to the sun."

This is a "find the sentence" question. You even underlined "waggle run" in the question. Next step: find those words in the passage and read that sentence.

Q9
You: blankCorrect: D By performing a longer waggle run

Last sentence of paragraph 3: "The length of the waggle run signals distance, with a longer run meaning a longer flight."

Choice A is the opposite. Tight circles mean the food is close to the hive.

Q10
You: blankCorrect: A show that his conclusion came from patient observation

The passage says "After years of marking individual bees and tracking where they flew, he concluded that the dance carried specific information." The years of work come right before the conclusion, so the detail shows the conclusion was earned through long, careful watching.

Q11
You: blankCorrect: B how small an animal can be and still communicate complex information

The last sentence says "a tiny animal with a brain the size of a grass seed can describe a place it has been." The author puts a very small brain next to a very impressive skill to make you feel the surprise.

Q12
You: blankCorrect: A scientific questions can remain open even after a major discovery

Read the whole sentence: researchers "still argue about exactly how much of the message the followers use, but no one disputes the central discovery." The big finding is accepted, and smaller questions are still open.

B, D, and E all say the discovery is wrong. The passage says "no one disputes" it, so you can cross out all three.

Passage 4 · Emily Roebling and the Brooklyn Bridge

Q22
You: A that she preferred public attentionCorrect: B that her role extended beyond carrying messages

At first Emily carried instructions between the sickroom and the bridge. Then officials wanted to replace Washington, and "Emily spoke on his behalf, and he kept the job." She was now defending him and changing a decision, which is much more than being a messenger.

Nothing in the passage says she liked attention. If you can't point to a line that supports a choice, cross it out.

Passage 5 · Marisol and the cello

Q27
You: D practiced her cello more than usualCorrect: A hoped repetition would make the bad news bearable

The sentence is a comparison: "She practiced the news the way she practiced a difficult passage—over and over, slowly, hoping it would become easier." She is not playing the cello here. She is going over the bad news in her mind again and again, hoping it will hurt less.

Trap: the word "practiced" makes you think of music. Ask what she is practicing. The sentence says she practiced the news.

Passage 6 · Why schools should teach cooking

Q31
You: A saving money over a lifetimeCorrect: E developing flexible, practical thinking

Paragraph 2 gives the money reason. Then paragraph 3 begins "But the stronger case is not financial. Cooking teaches a kind of thinking that is hard to learn from a textbook." The author tells you which reason is stronger.

Signal words like but, however, and the stronger case show you where the author's real point is. Saving money is in the passage, but it is the weaker reason.

Q33
You: B choosing chores instead of homeworkCorrect: C moving from being helped to being helpful

"That reversal" points back to the sentence just before it: a fourteen-year-old who can cook "is not simply being helped by her family; she is helping them." The thing that reverses is who helps whom.

When a question asks what "that" or "this" refers to, read the sentence right before it. Homework is never mentioned in paragraph 4.

Passage 7 · Crows that remember faces

Q37
You: A Crows mobbed the dangerous mask years later.Correct: E Birds that had never been captured joined the scolding.

The question asks for proof that crows learn from one another. Paragraph 3: "many of the scolders had never been captured at all. Young crows and neighbors were evidently learning the threat from other birds rather than from experience." Birds that were never caught could only know about the mask from other crows.

Your choice is a real finding, but it shows something else: that the memory lasts a long time. Right detail, wrong question.

Q40
You: A certain that crows give giftsCorrect: D careful to note that an interpretation is disputed

The sentence says some observers, "though not all researchers, interpret" the objects as gifts. The author is telling you that experts disagree. That is being careful, which is the opposite of being certain.

Words like some, though not all, may, and interpret are caution words. When you see them, the author is not claiming something is a fact.

Section 3 · Verbal

Part One · Synonyms

Q10
You: blankCorrect: D request

A plea is a serious, emotional request. "She made a plea for help." It comes from the verb plead, which means to beg.

You could cross out "insult" and "reward" just by thinking of someone pleading. That is enough to make a guess worth it.

Q23
You: A mockCorrect: C respect deeply

To revere someone is to respect and admire them very much. Related words: reverence (deep respect) and Reverend (a title of respect for a minister).

To mock is to make fun of someone, which is almost the opposite. The SSAT often puts an opposite among the choices.

Q25
You: blankCorrect: A thinly scattered

Sparse means spread out with only a little in each place. "Sparse trees" means only a few trees, far apart. "Sparse hair" means thin hair.

Choice C, "crowded," is the opposite. Sparse and dense are antonyms.

Part Two · Analogies

Q34
You: D goatCorrect: E cow

Bridge: a cub is a baby lion. A calf is a baby cow.

A baby goat is called a kid. A baby sheep is a lamb, a baby deer is a fawn, and a baby horse is a foal.

Q36
You: D waterCorrect: C rest

Bridge: hunger is fixed by food. Fatigue means being very tired, so fatigue is fixed by rest.

Water fixes thirst. This one came down to knowing the word fatigue.

Q42
You: E giftCorrect: B sticks

Bridge: a bouquet is a bunch of flowers tied together. A bundle is a bunch of sticks tied together.

A gift may be wrapped up, but a bundle is not "a bunch of gifts." Make the bridge say what the thing is made of.

Q45
You: B runnerCorrect: A conductor

Bridge: a referee uses a whistle to direct the players. A conductor uses a baton to direct the orchestra.

Smart thought, because a relay runner does carry a baton. But the runner only carries it and passes it on. The referee uses the whistle to control a group, and only the conductor does that with a baton.

Q46
You: blankCorrect: B sawmill

Bridge: flour is made in a mill. Lumber is made in a sawmill.

A forest is where trees grow, but trees are cut into lumber at the sawmill. A carpenter uses lumber and does not make it.

Q47
You: C shineBest answer: A signal

Bridge: the purpose of camouflage is to hide. The purpose of a beacon is to signal. A beacon is a light or fire set up to warn or guide people, like a lighthouse.

A beacon does shine, but shining is how it works. Signaling is what it is for. The first pair is about purpose, so the second pair must be too.

Q49
You: blankCorrect: D desert

Bridge: an island is a piece of land surrounded by water. An oasis is a green place with water surrounded by desert.

Palms, springs, and camels are things you might find at an oasis. The bridge asks what surrounds it.

Q51
You: blankCorrect: A snow

Bridge: an epidemic is a severe, widespread outbreak of disease. A blizzard is a severe, widespread fall of snow.

E is the trap. A blizzard is a kind of storm, but an epidemic is not "a kind of disease." It is a lot of disease all at once.

Q53
You: blankCorrect: D rubber

Bridge: glass is a well-known brittle material (it breaks instead of bending). Rubber is a well-known elastic material (it stretches and springs back).

Q54
You: blankCorrect: B doubt

Bridge: an optimist is a person full of hope. A pessimist expects things to go badly, so a pessimist is a person full of doubt.

You can cross out joy and patience right away because they are positive feelings.

Q55
You: C branchCorrect: E moth

Bridge: an oak comes out of an acorn. A moth comes out of a cocoon.

A cocoon may hang from a branch, but that is where it is, not what it becomes. Keep the same direction: small beginning → what it turns into.

Q56
You: blankCorrect: B final version

Bridge: a rehearsal is the practice that comes before the performance. A draft is the practice writing that comes before the final version.

Q57
You: blankCorrect: D maps

Bridge: a dictionary is a book of words. An atlas is a book of maps.

Countries are shown on the maps, but the atlas itself is made of maps. Globes are a different object, not what is inside an atlas.

Q58
You: blankCorrect: E body

Bridge: fuel gives an engine the energy to run. Food gives the body the energy to run.

Q59
You: blankCorrect: C tree

Bridge: an anchor holds a ship in place. A root holds a tree in place.

Soil is what the root holds on to, the same way the sea floor is what the anchor holds on to. The question asks for what is being held.

Q60
You: E drawBest answer: A write

Bridge: to mumble is to speak in an unclear, careless way. To scribble is to write in a messy, careless way.

Little kids do scribble drawings, so E is not silly. But mumble and speak are both about words, and scribble and write are both about words too, so A is the closer match.
