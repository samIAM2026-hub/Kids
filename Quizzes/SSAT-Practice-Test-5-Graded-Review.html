Section 2 · Math — what went wrong

Every miss, worked through step by step

Math 8Angle between lines l and mmarked E (55°)answer: D (50°)
The rays go in order from left to right: j, k, l, m. You are told j to l is 55° and j to k is 25°, so k to l = 55 − 25 = 30°. You are also told k to m is 80°, and that 80° is made of k-to-l plus l-to-m. So l to m = 80 − 30 = 50°. Marking 55° is picking a number that is already printed in the question — the test puts those numbers in the choices on purpose. Label every gap on the figure before you look at the answers.
Math 10Order A, B, C from least to greatestmarked C (B < A < C)answer: D (C < A < B)
Don't compare letters — turn them into numbers first. C = 4. Then A = C + 3 = 7 and B = 2C = 8. Now it's easy: 4, 7, 8, so C < A < B. Choosing "B < A < C" would mean B is the smallest, but B is the doubled one, so B is always the biggest here. Write the number next to each letter on the page.
Math 13Four lines, longest to shortestmarked C (C, B, A, D)answer: E (B, C, A, D)
Good instinct crossing off A, B and D — but C and B got swapped. C spans the full width of the box but is almost flat, so its length is just a bit more than the width. B spans the full width and most of the height, so it is the long corner-to-corner diagonal — longer than C. Then A (a shorter diagonal) and D (the short steep one at the right). Order: B, C, A, D. Rule of thumb: a slanted line always beats a flat line that covers the same left-to-right distance.
Math 17Which is equal to 8/20?marked D (8%)answer: E (40%)
Simplify first: 8/20 = 2/5. To turn a fraction into a percent, make the bottom 100: 2/5 = 40/100 = 40%. Marking 8% is taking the 8 off the top of the fraction and sticking a % sign on it — that is exactly the trap answer the test wants. Quick check: 8 out of 20 is nearly half, and half is 50%, so the answer has to be somewhere near 50 — 8% is nowhere close. Sanity-check the size of a percent before you circle it.
Math 25Ratio of shaded to unshadedmarked E (16/11)answer: D (7/5)
The grid is 6 across × 5 down = 30 squares. Count the shaded ones row by row, and count a square the diagonal cuts through as one half:
Row 1: 1  ·  Row 2: 2 whole + a half = 2½  ·  Row 3: 3 whole + a half = 3½  ·  Row 4: 4 whole + a half = 4½  ·  Row 5: 6.
Shaded = 1 + 2.5 + 3.5 + 4.5 + 6 = 17½. Unshaded = 30 − 17.5 = 12½. The ratio 17.5 : 12.5 doubles to 35 : 25, which cancels to 7 : 5. The halves are the whole question — count them, don't round them.

Math 18Rectangle, perimeter 52, width 4 shorter than lengthleft blankanswer: B (11 cm)
Perimeter = 2 × (length + width), so length + width = 52 ÷ 2 = 26. The width is 4 less than the length, so length + (length − 4) = 26 → 2×length = 30 → length = 15, and width = 11. Always halve the perimeter first — it turns a two-step problem into a one-step one.
Math 19Which set is in order, least to greatest?left blankanswer: E
Turn everything into a decimal: 55% = 0.55, 0.58, 3/5 = 0.60, 66% = 0.66, 2/3 = 0.667, 0.70. In order that's 55%, 0.58, 3/5, 66%, 2/3, 0.70 — choice (E). The whole question turns on 2/3 vs 66%: two-thirds is 66.67%, so it is bigger than 66%. Convert everything to decimals before you compare, every time.
Math 20Negative N multiplied by a number greater than 1left blankanswer: A (less than N)
When a question is all letters, put in a real number. Let N = −2 and multiply by 3: you get −6, which is further left on the number line than −2. Try ×1.5: −3, still further left. A negative number multiplied by anything bigger than 1 always slides away from zero, so the answer is always less than N.
Math 21Biggest gap between men and women at the gymleft blankanswer: B (Saturday)
Read the two bars for each day and subtract: Mon 20 vs 20 = 0, Tue 40 vs 60 = 20, Wed 20 vs 50 = 30, Thu 60 vs 70 = 10, Fri 50 vs 30 = 20, Sat 70 vs 30 = 40, Sun 50 vs 20 = 30. Saturday wins. Write the difference above each pair of bars — it takes 20 seconds and removes all the guessing.
Math 22How many more men on Saturday than Monday?left blankanswer: D (50)
Only the dark bars (men): Saturday 70, Monday 20. 70 − 20 = 50. Ignore the women's bars completely — the trap here is subtracting the wrong pair.
Math 23Average women, Monday through Fridayleft blankanswer: D (46)
Women Mon–Fri: 20 + 60 + 50 + 70 + 30 = 230. Five days, so 230 ÷ 5 = 46. Note it says Monday through Friday — five days, not seven. Weekend bars are decoys.
Math 24Equilateral triangle, perimeter 12left blankanswer: D (4 inches)
"Sides of equal length" means all three sides are the same, so just divide: 12 ÷ 3 = 4. This is the easiest question in the section — it was worth a full point and it cost nothing but the time to read it. Skim ahead for the short questions before the clock runs out.
Pattern in the math: the five misses are not topics you don't know — they are one skipped step. Subtracting to find the gap (Q8), substituting the number for the letter (Q10), simplifying before converting (Q17), counting halves (Q25). And then seven questions at the end that never got read at all. Spend the last two minutes filling bubbles, not on the hardest question.

Every miss, with the line from the passage that decides it

Reading 3Why the U.S. has few night marketsmarked E (small businesses)answer: D (government regulation)
The passage answers this in one sentence, lines 20–23: "This is likely because government regulations in many urbanized areas limit the operations of informal vendors." The words "small businesses" do appear — but as something night markets support, not as a reason there are few of them. The trap is picking a phrase you remember seeing rather than the one that answers the question asked.
Reading 5All are benefits of night markets EXCEPTmarked B (tourism)answer: C (after-hours access to name-brand merchandise)
On an EXCEPT question, find the four that ARE in the text and the leftover is your answer. Small businesses — line 30. ✓ Tourism — line 32. ✓ Social cohesion — line 16. ✓ Flexibility to adapt to people's needs — lines 18–19. ✓ Name-brand merchandise appears nowhere; night markets are the opposite of brand-name stores. Tourism was crossed out even though it is right there in the last paragraph.
Reading 6The passage is primarily concerned with explaining…marked C (hibernation habits)answer: E (extending its winter range into colder climates)
Torpor gets one paragraph; the whole rest of the passage is about the bird now wintering far north of San Francisco. A "primarily concerned with" question wants the big idea, not the most interesting detail. Also — the passage never says hibernation. Torpor is a nightly drop in body temperature; hibernation is a whole winter asleep. They are not the same thing.
Reading 10The loss of forest habitat to urban development has…marked D (reduced its food supply)answer: B (helped it extend its habitat)
Lines 15–18 say it straight out: "Urban development has replaced evergreen forests with lush, diverse flower gardens, ideal for these birds…" In real life, losing forest is bad news — and that is exactly why (D) feels right. But this passage says the opposite. Answer what the passage says, not what you already believe. That single habit is worth several points a test.
Reading 14"He paused, breathing steadily in the falling sunlight"marked D (how he read the thoughts)answer: B (the patient character of the older monk)
Look at what sits either side of that sentence: the younger monk has "stormy thoughts", and the older monk is breathing steadily in the sunlight. That contrast is a picture of a calm, patient man. Breathing steadily doesn't explain mind-reading — nothing in the story does. When a sentence describes a person's calm actions, it is usually there to show you what that person is like. (This one is a judgement call — B and D are both arguable, but B is the standard SSAT answer.)
Reading 23"tome" (line 7) most nearly meansmarked D (scripture)answer: A (volume)
A tome is a big, heavy book — a volume. Test it by putting each choice back in the sentence: "one centuries-old volume, called The Manual of Clinical Practice and Emergency Remedies" works perfectly. Scripture means a religious text, and this is a medical manual. The words "ancient Chinese medicine" nearby made scripture feel right — that's the trap.
Reading 31The word "them" in line 8 refers tomarked A (Parisians)answer: B (readers)
The sentence: "Victor Hugo takes readers deep into the Parisian underworld, immersing them in a battle between good and evil…" Whoever is being immersed is whoever was taken — the readers. For a pronoun question, read backwards to the nearest noun that makes sense doing that action, and then read the sentence again with your choice swapped in.
Reading 38The passage suggests that…marked E (disasters more common in Kyoto)answer: D (Kyoto had once been more prosperous)
The passage says Buddhist statues and altars were "smashed…piled up on the side of the road and sold as kindling" and that no one repairs the gate any more. Things can only fall apart if they were once in better shape — so the text implies Kyoto used to be more prosperous. (E) is a comparison with other cities that the passage never makes. If a choice compares two things and the passage only talks about one of them, cross it off.

Section 4 · Synonyms — the 12 misses

Each word, what it really means, and the hook to remember it by

Verbal 2PRESUMEmarked B (implore)answer: E (assume)
Presume = to take something for granted without proof. Look at the two words side by side: pre-sume and as-sume — the "sume" part means take, and both mean taking something as true. Implore means to beg desperately.
Verbal 3VIRTUOUSmarked C (grateful)answer: D (honorable)
A virtue is a good moral quality (honesty, kindness, courage), so virtuous = morally good = honorable. Grateful is about being thankful, which is a feeling, not a moral standard.
Verbal 4DOMESTICmarked D (terrestrial)answer: C (familial)
Domestic = to do with the home or family — domestic chores, a domestic cat, domestic life. It comes from domus, Latin for house. Familial = to do with family. Terrestrial means to do with the earth (think extra-terrestrial = from beyond earth).
Verbal 6INSINUATEmarked B (connect)answer: A (imply)
To insinuate is to hint at something unpleasant without actually saying it — "He insinuated that I had cheated." That is exactly what imply means. It's a sneaky, sideways word.
Verbal 9KINDLEmarked A (welcome)answer: D (ignite)
To kindle a fire is to start it — and kindling is the name for the little sticks you use to light one. Ignite = set alight. It probably looked like "kind", but the two words are unrelated.
Verbal 10FASTENmarked C (hasten)answer: A (affix)
Fasten = attach firmly (fasten your seatbelt) = affix. Hasten is fasten with an h — and it means to hurry. This is a shape-of-the-word slip: they look almost identical but share no meaning. Read the first letter deliberately on every choice.
Verbal 14DISSENTmarked C (repellant)answer: B (opposition)
Dissent = disagreeing with the majority, especially publicly. Break it up: dis- (against) + -sent (feel) = to feel against → opposition. Don't mix it up with descent, which means going down.
Verbal 15DEFYmarked C (damage)answer: D (challenge)
To defy is to openly refuse to obey — "she defied the rule". That is a challenge to whoever made the rule. It has nothing to do with breaking objects. Related word: defiant.
Verbal 17ORIGINATEmarked E (retrieve)answer: B (derive)
Originate = to begin, or to come from a source — the origin is where something starts. Derive means to come from a source too ("the word derives from Latin"). Retrieve means to fetch something back, which is the opposite direction.
Verbal 19OBSCUREmarked A (involve)answer: C (incomprehensible)
Obscure = hard to see, or hard to understand — "an obscure explanation". Incomprehensible means impossible to understand. Careful: choice (D) uncover is the deliberate opposite trap, since to obscure something is to hide it.
Verbal 20REMORSEmarked E (melancholy)answer: C (guilt)
Remorse is the sick, sorry feeling you get after you have done something wrong — that is guilt. Melancholy is plain sadness with no wrongdoing attached. Close, but remorse always has a mistake behind it. Related word: remorseful.
Verbal 22CORDIALmarked C (numerical)answer: A (neighborly)
Cordial = warm and friendly — "a cordial welcome". It comes from cor, Latin for heart (same root as core). Warm-hearted → neighborly. "Numerical" probably came from cardinal (cardinal numbers) — different word.

Section 4 · Analogies — the 7 misses

Every analogy is won by building the sentence first

Verbal 33Red is to maroon as…marked D (luster : gleam)answer: A (organ : heart)
Build the sentence: "Maroon is one specific kind of red." General → specific. Now test: "A heart is one specific kind of organ." ✓ Luster and gleam are just two words for shine — that's a synonym pair, not general-to-specific. And watch (B): olive is to green is the right relationship backwards (olive is the specific one). Direction always matters.
Verbal 36Hurt is to devastate as…marked B (shock : surprise)answer: E (inhabit : infest)
Sentence: "To devastate is to hurt to an extreme degree." Mild → extreme. Shock : surprise runs extreme → mild, so it is the right idea pointing the wrong way. Inhabit (simply live in) → infest (overrun a place) goes mild → extreme. ✓ When two choices share a relationship, the direction decides it.
Verbal 38Cheese is to sandwich as…marked E (sheet : mattress)answer: D (apple : pie)
Sentence: "Cheese is an ingredient inside a sandwich." Apple is an ingredient inside a pie. ✓ A sheet goes on top of a mattress — it covers it, it isn't part of it. Close, but "inside" beats "on top of". (A judgement call worth checking against the book, but "ingredient of" is the tighter match.)
Verbal 45Confidence is to doubtful as…marked C (charity : poor)answer: A (grace : gawky)
Sentence: "Someone doubtful has no confidence." These are opposites. Gawky means clumsy — someone gawky has no grace. ✓ Charity and poor go together; they don't clash. Nice work crossing off four choices here — the elimination was right, the last step just needed the opposites test.
Verbal 50Home is to shelter as…marked A (point : graph)answer: B (medicine : treat)
Sentence: "The job of a home is to shelter you." The job of medicine is to treat you. ✓ Here's the shortcut: "shelter" is being used as a verb, so the second word of the right answer must be a verb too. Graph is a noun, so (A) can't match the shape.
Verbal 59Lumpy is to smooth as…marked D (heroic : coward)answer: B (tumultuous : still)
Sentence: "Lumpy is the opposite of smooth" — and both are adjectives. Tumultuous (stormy, wild) is the opposite of still, and both are adjectives. ✓ Heroic is an adjective but coward is a noun (the adjective would be cowardly), so the pair is the wrong shape. Check that both pairs use the same parts of speech.
Verbal 60Fearless is to timid as…marked E (apprehensive : fearsome)answer: A (modest : vain)
Opposites again. Modest (humble about yourself) is the opposite of vain (full of yourself). ✓ Apprehensive means nervous; fearsome means frightening — two fear-words, but not opposites. Crossing off (B) fearful : nervous and (C) courageous : brave was exactly right — both are synonym pairs. You got down to two and then picked the one that only looked like it was about fear.

Section 4 · Analogies — the 13 left blank

The sentence that solves each one

Verbal 31Antithesis is to contrastleft blankanswer: D (uninterested : bored)
An antithesis IS a contrast — they mean the same thing. So look for a synonym pair: uninterested and bored. ✓
Verbal 32Objective is to bias as thoughtful is to…left blankanswer: B (carelessness)
"Someone objective has no bias." So: "Someone thoughtful has no carelessness." ✓ The pattern is adjective : the noun it lacks.
Verbal 39Vicious is to aggressiveleft blankanswer: D (repulsive : unpleasant)
"Vicious is extremely aggressive." Extreme → mild. "Repulsive is extremely unpleasant." ✓
Verbal 40Maneuver is to exercise as excerpt is to…left blankanswer: E (passage)
A maneuver is a kind of exercise (a military drill). An excerpt is a passage — a piece taken out of a longer text. Near-synonyms both ways. ✓
Verbal 42Deplete is to expend as evade is to…left blankanswer: A (shun)
Deplete and expend both mean to use up. So we want a synonym for evade (avoid) — shun means to deliberately avoid. ✓
Verbal 46Statue is to memorializeleft blankanswer: B (museum : preserve)
"The purpose of a statue is to memorialize." "The purpose of a museum is to preserve." ✓ Purpose analogies are common — ask what is this thing FOR?
Verbal 47Pragmatist is to realistic as instigator is to…left blankanswer: E (troublemaking)
A pragmatist is realistic by definition. An instigator is someone who stirs up trouble — so an instigator is troublemaking by definition. ✓
Verbal 51Star is to constellationleft blankanswer: C (spectator : audience)
"One star is a member of a constellation." "One spectator is a member of an audience." ✓ Member → group.
Verbal 52Strict is to tyrannicalleft blankanswer: E (pleasant : exquisite)
"Tyrannical is strict taken to an extreme." Mild → extreme. "Exquisite is pleasant taken to an extreme." ✓
Verbal 55Pandemonium is to disorderleft blankanswer: E (overhaul : modification)
Pandemonium is extreme disorder (total chaos). An overhaul is an extreme modification — you rebuild the whole thing. ✓
Verbal 56Content is to comfortableleft blankanswer: C (dissatisfied : disgruntled)
Content and comfortable are near-synonyms (both quietly happy). Dissatisfied and disgruntled are near-synonyms (both quietly unhappy). ✓
Verbal 57Acknowledge is to endorseleft blankanswer: A (dismiss : forsake)
To acknowledge is a mild yes; to endorse is a strong yes. Mirror it on the negative side: to dismiss is a mild no; to forsake is a strong no (abandon completely). ✓
Verbal 58District is to cityleft blankanswer: A (atom : molecule)
"A district is a part of a city." "An atom is a part of a molecule." ✓ Part → whole, in that order.
The one habit that fixes analogies: before you read a single choice, write a short sentence linking the two words — "a district is a part of a city". Then read each choice into that same sentence. Also check two things you kept missing: direction (mild→extreme, not extreme→mild) and word type (adjective with adjective, verb with verb). You are already good at eliminating — the crossing-out on questions 45 and 60 was excellent. The sentence turns a two-way guess into a certainty.

Math 6The 52nd flower in a repeating patternmarked D (marigold)answer: B (buttercup)
The pattern is 5 flowers long: daisy, buttercup, rose, marigold, petunia — then it starts again. So divide and keep the remainder: 52 ÷ 5 = 10 remainder 2. Ten full rounds finish at flower 50, so the 52nd is the 2nd flower — buttercup. Marigold is 4th, which would be flowers 4, 9, 14… 54. For any repeating pattern: divide by the length of the pattern, and the remainder tells you the position.
Math 7Most wins while still losing half the seasonmarked C (5)answer: B (1)
Work with the losses, not the wins. Half of 20 games = 10 losses allowed. They have lost 4, so they can lose 6 more. They have played 9 + 4 = 13 games, so 7 games remain. If 6 of those 7 must be losses, only 1 can be a win. The question sounds like "how many can they win in total" but it says "during the rest of the season". Underline the phrase that says which part of the season you're counting.
Math 9Largest integer with −9 < x < −1marked B (−8)answer: C (−2)
On the number line, bigger means further right — and with negatives that is the one closest to zero. So −2 is much bigger than −8. The allowed values are −8, −7, … −2, and the largest is −2. (−1 is not allowed because the sign is <, not ≤.) With negative numbers, "largest" feels backwards — sketch a quick number line every time.
Math 13If 5n is a whole number, n could be…marked C (5.50)answer: E (7.20)

Math 17Perimeter of the hexagon-plus-square figureleft blankanswer: B (40 units)
The hexagon has 6 sides of 5 = 30, but the side AD is shared with the square and sits inside the shape, so it is not on the outside edge: 30 − 5 = 25. The square then adds its other 3 sides: 3 × 5 = 15. Total 25 + 15 = 40. Trace the outline with your pencil — any line you never touch is not part of the perimeter.

Math 23Longest line that fits inside the circleleft blankanswer: B (7.5)
The longest line you can draw inside a circle is the diameter (straight through the centre). Circumference = π × diameter, so diameter = 25.13 ÷ 3.14 = 8. The closest choice to 8 is 7.5. Two facts do the whole question: longest line = diameter, and C = πd.
Math 24Who might run faster than Erin?left blankanswer: B (Abigail)
Write the two chains out: Abigail > Francis > Charles, and Erin > Paulie > Francis. Erin beats Paulie, Francis and Charles for certain. But Abigail is never compared to Erin — she is only known to beat Francis. So Abigail is the one who might be faster. The word "might" is asking who is left unmeasured.
Math 25If xy = 1, which MUST be true?left blankanswer: B (x = 1/y)
Divide both sides by y: xy = 1 → x = 1/y. That is always true. Check the others with an example like x = 4, y = 0.25: x is not equal to y, and x = 1 and y = 1 is only one case out of many. "MUST be true" means find one counter-example and the choice is dead.
