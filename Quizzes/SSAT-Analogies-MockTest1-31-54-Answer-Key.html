The 3 misses, explained

Each one starts with the sentence that should have been written first

34 · Paper : novelchose D · color is to paletteanswer: B · canvas is to portrait
The bridge sentence: "Paper is the material a novel is printed on."
Now read every choice into that exact sentence, in that exact order. Canvas is the material a portrait is painted on. ✓ Perfect — same job, different art form.
Why D was tempting, and why it fails. Color and palette are both art words, and paper and novel are both book words, so the choice feels parallel. But feel is not a bridge. Read it back: "Color is the material a palette is painted on"? No — a palette is the tray that holds the colors. That is a completely different relationship, and it is backwards as well: the palette holds the color, so if anything the pair is reversed.
Check the others too, because they are all near-misses on purpose: paint : brush is material and tool. Back : chair is part and whole. Person : poll is barely a pair at all. Only one choice survives the sentence.
This one wasn't circled — it went by at full speed and felt fine. That is exactly what the topic trap is designed to do. Warren's rule: same subject area is not a relationship. If the only thing linking your choice to the stem is "they're both about art" or "they're both about books," it is the trap.
41 · Beg : borrow :: offer : ?chose E · lenderanswer: B · lend
The bridge sentence: "You beg because you want to borrow — and you offer because you are willing to lend."
Beg and borrow are the two halves of one side of a transaction: the person who wants the thing. Offer and lend are the two halves of the other side: the person who has it. Beg is the asking, borrow is the taking; offer is the giving-out, lend is the giving.
The part-of-speech mirror would have killed E before you even thought about meaning. Look at the stem: beg and borrow are both verbs. So the answer has to be a verb. Lender is a person — a noun. Security is a noun. Bank is a noun. That leaves repay and lend, and now it is a two-way choice you'd win every time.
Three of the five choices die on word form alone. That is the single cheapest point on the verbal section: glance at the stem's word type before you read the choices. It costs one second and it usually deletes two or three options for free.
The circle was right. Question 41 was flagged as uncertain — good instinct. The pause just needs to be spent on the mirror check, not on re-reading the choices hoping one will start to look better.
52 · Receive : admit :: settle : ?chose B · remainanswer: E · adjust
The bridge sentence: "To receive someone into a place and to admit someone into a place mean the same thing."
This is a plain synonym stem hiding behind a second meaning. Admit has two lives: "to confess" (admit you broke it) and "to let in" (the club admits members). Next to receive, it is clearly the let-in meaning. So the bridge is simply: these two words mean the same thing.
Then the answer just has to be a word that means the same as settle. Settle also has two lives — "to come to rest" and "to sort out a disagreement." Adjust matches the second one: you settle a dispute, you adjust a claim, and in both cases you are putting something right.
Why B is the trap. Remain connects to "settle" only through the first meaning — settle down, stay put. It is an association, not a synonym: "settle" and "remain" are not interchangeable in any sentence. Test it. "They settled the argument" ≠ "They remained the argument." The swap test kills it instantly.
Warren's rule: on a synonym stem, ask can I swap the two words in a real sentence? If you can't swap them, they aren't synonyms — no matter how related they feel.

The debatable one · #45

Read this one slowly — it teaches the single most valuable habit on the page

45 · Milliliter : quartchose B · foot is to yardanswer: C · millimeter is to yard ✱
First bridge (loose): "A milliliter is a smaller unit than a quart."
With that loose sentence, three choices survive: pint : quart, foot : yard, and inch : yard. All three are smaller-unit-to-larger-unit. When more than one choice survives, the sentence was too loose — and the fix is never to guess between the survivors. The fix is to tighten the bridge.
Tightened bridge: "A milliliter is a tiny metric unit; a quart is a much larger non-metric unit — they're about a thousand times apart, and they come from two different systems."
Now run the survivors through it again. Pint : quart — same system, only twice as big. Dead. Foot : yard — same system, only three times as big. Dead. Inch : yard — same system, 36 times. Dead. Millimeter : yard — metric to non-metric, and roughly 914 times apart. That is the stem's exact shape.
The ✱ is honest: some answer keys accept foot : yard because the loose bridge does fit it. But on a real SSAT, when two choices both fit your sentence, the tighter fit is the intended one — the test writer put millimeter : yard on the list precisely so the scale gap and the system crossing would matter. Don't lose sleep over the point. Do keep the habit.
Warren's rule, and it is the big one: if two choices survive your sentence, your sentence is too loose — go back and add one more detail. Never pick between two survivors by feel. Adding a detail takes three seconds and turns a coin flip into a certainty.

Bridge: "Fundamental means something that really matters; frivolous means something that doesn't matter at all — opposites about seriousness." Truth versus nonsense is the same axis: substance versus emptiness. ✓
Notice you added the axis — "opposites about seriousness," not just "opposites." That extra word is what beat strange : common (opposites about how usual something is) and nonfiction : fact (not opposites at all). That is the method working. It is the same move that would have won #45.

Warren's method · the bridge, in four steps

Every miss on this paper is fixed by one of these four steps. The whole routine takes about twelve seconds a question.

  1. Cover the choices and say a sentence with a real verb. Not "they're related" — an actual sentence: "A pilot steers an airplane." "Paper is what a novel is printed on." If you can't build the sentence, you don't have the relationship yet, and no amount of staring at the choices will hand it to you.
  2. Check the word type before you read the choices. Verb : verb stem needs a verb : verb answer. This one-second glance deleted three of the five choices on #41 for free — including the one that was circled.
  3. Read each choice back into your sentence, in the same order. Same order matters: "the palette holds the color" is backwards from "paper carries the novel," and that reversal is what cost #34. If the sentence sounds wrong when you say it out loud, it is wrong.
  4. Two survivors means the sentence is too loose — tighten it once. Add a detail: "about seriousness," "about shape," "a thousand times smaller, and a different system." No survivors means it was too tight — loosen it once, then move on. Never choose between survivors by feel.
The four traps that took points on this exact paper:
  • The topic trap (#34) — the choice is from the same subject area but a different relationship. Same topic is not a relationship.
  • The wrong word form (#41) — the right idea in the wrong part of speech. Lend, not lender.
  • The loose association (#52) — a word that goes with the stem instead of a word that means the same. Use the swap test: if you can't swap them in a sentence, they aren't synonyms.
  • The too-loose bridge (#45) — the sentence fits three choices, so it decides nothing. Tighten it.

The ten-minute daily rhythm. Twenty analogies a day beats one whole practice test a week.
  • Say the bridge out loud for every single one, even the easy ones. Especially the easy ones — that's where the habit gets built cheaply.
  • Log every miss with one of five causes: topic trap · reversed order · wrong word form · too loose · didn't know the word. Four of those five are method, and method is free points.
  • Circle the ones you're unsure of — then write the sentence in the margin. The circle is already a good habit; the sentence is what turns it into a right answer.
  • Once a week, redo only your misses from the same paper. Don't chase fresh questions until the old ones come out clean.
