🧩 Variables and Expressions

This week a letter is just a number you haven’t been told yet. Factor it out, expand it, measure a shape with it, take its square root — and then prove that two different-looking expressions are really the same number.

1 · Factor out the GCF — all of it

Factoring is the distributive law run backwards. You look for the biggest thing that divides every term, pull it out front, and write what is left in brackets. “Biggest” has two parts:

PartHow to find itIn 12x6 + 18x4
The numberThe greatest common factor of the coefficientsGCF of 12 and 18 is 6
Each letterThe smallest power of that letter in any termx6 and x4 → x4

So the factor out front is 6x4, and 12x6 + 18x4 = 6x4(2x2 + 3). Why the smallest power? Because x4 fits inside x6, but x6 does not fit inside x4.

Example
Factor 8y9 + 12y6 completely.
  1. Number: the GCF of 8 and 12 is 4.
  2. Letter: the powers are 9 and 6, so the smallest is y6.
  3. Pull out 4y6: 8y9 ÷ 4y6 = 2y3 and 12y6 ÷ 4y6 = 3.
  4. Check by expanding: 4y6 · 2y3 + 4y6 · 3 = 8y9 + 12y6 ✓
Answer: 4y6(2y3 + 3)
Example · three terms
Factor 10b6 − 15b4 + 35b2 as far as you can.
  1. Number: GCF of 10, 15 and 35 is 5.
  2. Letter: powers 6, 4 and 2 — the smallest is b2.
  3. Divide each term by 5b2: 2b4, −3b2, +7. Keep each sign with its term.
  4. Look inside the bracket: 2, 3 and 7 share nothing, and the 7 has no b. Nothing more comes out, so you are done.
Answer: 5b2(2b4 − 3b2 + 7)
⚠️ Trap

On Problem 1 your first answer was 3(3x7 + 2x5). It is true, but it is not finished: both terms in the bracket still contain x5. AoPS only accepts it when nothing common is left inside.

And you can never squash 9a4 + 3a3 − 21a into one term like −9a6. Different powers are unlike terms — they cannot be added. Factoring changes the shape of an expression, not its value.

Remember

Two checks every time: (1) expand your answer — you must get the original back; (2) look inside the bracket — if every term still shares a number or a letter, keep going.

2 · Expand, then collect like terms

You nailed Problems 3 and 4, so this is a quick warm-up. Multiply the outside term by every term inside, then add up the terms with the same power. The danger is always the minus sign in front of a bracket — it belongs to the outside term.

Example
Simplify 5(2k2 − k + 3) − 3k(4 − 2k).
  1. First bracket: 10k2 − 5k + 15.
  2. Second bracket: the outside term is −3k. −3k · 4 = −12k and −3k · (−2k) = +6k2.
  3. All together: 10k2 − 5k + 15 − 12k + 6k2.
  4. Collect: k2 terms 10 + 6 = 16; k terms −5 − 12 = −17; number 15.
Answer: 16k2 − 17k + 15
⚠️ Trap

−3k · (−2k) is plus 6k2. Two negatives multiply to a positive. Writing the outside term with its sign, (−3k), before you multiply makes this hard to miss.

3 · Area puzzles: name one length, build the rest

In a diagram puzzle, you are given one length as a letter. Your job is to write every other length in terms of that letter, using the facts the picture gives you (“identical”, “square”). Then the area is just width × height.

The big shape above is a square. On the left are 6 identical rectangles, each of height h. On the right are 4 identical squares.

Example
In the picture above, find the area of one rectangle in terms of h.
  1. Stack of rectangles: 6 heights of h, so the side of the big square is 6h.
  2. The right column is also 6h tall and holds 4 equal squares, so each small square has side 6h4 = 3h2.
  3. The big square is 6h wide. The small square takes 3h2 of that, so a rectangle is 6h − 3h2 = 9h2 wide.
  4. Area = width × height = 9h2 · h.
Answer: 9h22
Example · same picture, new question
What is the perimeter of one of those rectangles?
  1. Two widths and two heights: 2 · 9h2 + 2 · h.
  2. = 9h + 2h = 11h.
Answer: 11h
⚠️ Trap

The rectangle is not as wide as the small square. The width is the big side minus the square’s side. Also watch area versus length: an area of a shape measured with h always has h2 in it.

Remember

If you want to check, give h a value. With h = 2: big side 12, small square 3, rectangle 9 × 2 = 18, and 9 · 222 = 18 ✓

4 · Square roots of powers: halve the exponent

√a16 asks: “what times itself makes a16?” Since a8 · a8 = a8+8 = a16, the answer is a8. So a square root halves the exponent.

When a number and letters are multiplied under the root, take the root of each factor separately: √49a6 = √49 · √a6 = 7a3. (On this page every letter stands for a positive number.)

Example
Simplify √225b12.
  1. √225 = 15, because 15 · 15 = 225.
  2. √b12 = b6: half of 12 is 6.
  3. Check: (15b6)2 = 225b12 ✓
Answer: 15b6
Example
Simplify √64x10y4.
  1. √64 = 8, √x10 = x5, √y4 = y2.
  2. Check: (8x5y2)2 = 64x10y4 ✓
Answer: 8x5y2
⚠️ Trap

√a16 is not a4. Don’t take the square root of the exponent — halve it. ((a4)2 = a8, not a16.)

And a root of a sum does not split: √9x2 + 16x2 is not 3x + 4x. Add first: √25x2 = 5x.

5 · Algebra fractions: factor first, cancel factors

To simplify a fraction with letters, factor the top and the bottom completely — this is where section 1 pays off. Then cancel a bracket only if it is a whole factor of the entire top and the entire bottom. The problem says “assume x is not …” because that value would make the bottom zero.

Example
Assume x ≠ 25. Simplify 20x2 − 8x10x − 4.
  1. Top: GCF is 4x, so 20x2 − 8x = 4x(5x − 2).
  2. Bottom: GCF is 2, so 10x − 4 = 2(5x − 2).
  3. (5x − 2) is a whole factor of both, and it is not zero, so it cancels: 4x2 = 2x.
Answer: 2x
Example · subtracting
Assume x ≠ −1. Simplify 5x + 3x + 1 − 2x − 22x + 2.
  1. Factor the second bottom: 2x + 2 = 2(x + 1). So the common bottom is 2(x + 1).
  2. Rewrite the first fraction: 2(5x + 3)2(x + 1) = 10x + 62(x + 1).
  3. Subtract the tops — the minus hits the whole second top: 10x + 6 − (2x − 2) = 8x + 8.
  4. 8x + 82(x + 1) = 8(x + 1)2(x + 1) = 4.
Answer: 4
⚠️ Trap

Cancel factors, never terms. x + 6x + 2 is not 62. Test x = 2: 84 = 2, not 3. The x’s are added, not multiplied, so they cannot cancel.

6 · Showing two expressions are equal

The writing problem this week gives you two expressions that look nothing alike and asks you to show they are equal. There are three jobs: check one value, explain where they break, and prove it for every other value. Here is a twin pair to practice on:

4x3 + 2x2 + 6x + 52x + 1 and 2x2 + 3 + 22x + 1
Example · one value
Show the two expressions are equal when x = 2.
  1. Strategy: work out each expression on its own, then compare.
  2. Left: top 32 + 8 + 12 + 5 = 57, bottom 2 · 2 + 1 = 5, so 575.
  3. Right: 8 + 3 + 25 = 11 25 = 575.
  4. Both are 575, so they are equal at x = 2.
Answer: Both equal 575
Example · where it breaks
Why are they not equal when x = −12?
  1. At x = −12, the bottom 2x + 1 = −1 + 1 = 0.
  2. Both expressions divide by 2x + 1, and you cannot divide by zero.
  3. So neither expression has a value there — two things that don’t exist can’t be equal numbers.
Answer: Both divide by zero at that x
Example · every other value
Show they are equal for every x ≠ −12.
  1. Strategy (say it in words first!): “I will start with the right side and rewrite it, one true step at a time, until it becomes the left side.”
  2. Since 2x + 1 ≠ 0, multiplying by 2x + 12x + 1 is multiplying by 1: 2x2 + 3 = (2x2 + 3)(2x + 1)2x + 1.
  3. Expand the top: (2x2 + 3)(2x + 1) = 4x3 + 2x2 + 6x + 3.
  4. Now the right side is 4x3 + 2x2 + 6x + 32x + 1 + 22x + 1 = 4x3 + 2x2 + 6x + 52x + 1, which is the left side. ✓
Answer: Every line is true, and the right side became the left side.
⚠️ Trap

Don’t start by writing “left = right” and then doing things to both sides. That line is the thing you are trying to prove — you would be writing an equation you don’t yet know is true. Start from one side and transform it.

And checking a few numbers is not a proof. Two values matching shows it might be true; only the algebra shows it is true for every x.

Remember

A good writing solution: (1) a sentence saying your plan, (2) a chain of lines that are each true, (3) a last sentence saying what you showed.
