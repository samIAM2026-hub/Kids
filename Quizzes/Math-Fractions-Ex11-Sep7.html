Exercise 11 — A Fraction is a Division

Sharing things out · writing 3 ÷ 2 as a fraction · changing improper fractions by dividing

Hi Grace! Exercise 11 teaches one single idea, and it is one of the most useful ideas in all of maths:

the line in a fraction means "divide".

Once you believe that, every question in this exercise is just a division you already know how to do. Read the top part, then take the test at the bottom — same questions, new numbers, 12 out of 15 (80%) to pass.

🍪 The big idea — the fraction line is a divide sign

Imagine 3 cookies shared fairly between 2 children. Each child gets one whole cookie, and then the last cookie is cut in half so they can share it. So each child gets one and a half cookies.

Written down, that story is 3 ÷ 2. And the answer, written as a fraction, is 32 — "three halves", because 3 cookies were each cut into halves and shared.

3 ÷ 2  =  3/2  =  1 and 1/2
Read it out loud like this 32 means "3 divided by 2". The top number is the thing being shared. The bottom number is how many people are sharing it. That works for every fraction you will ever meet: 74 means 7 shared between 4.

This also explains something you already knew: 66 = 6 ÷ 6 = 1. Six things shared between six people is one each. And 12 = 1 ÷ 2 — one cookie shared between two people is a half. It has always been a division.

1️⃣ Q1 — write the sharing picture as a fraction

In Q1 your book draws the whole pizzas at the top, then shows them cut up and put into equal piles underneath. Each pile is one person's share. Your job is to write down what one pile is worth.

1
Count the whole shapes at the top. That number goes on the top of your fraction.
2
Count the piles (the boxes) underneath. That number goes on the bottom.
3
Read off one pile to double-check: how many whole ones and how many bits does one person actually get?
Notice the pattern In every one of these, the shapes are cut into the same number of slices as there are piles. 3 shared between 2 → cut into halves. 5 shared between 3 → cut into thirds. 7 shared between 4 → cut into quarters. That is not a coincidence: the bottom number of the fraction is the number of people sharing.

2️⃣ Q2 — change an improper fraction by dividing

Now the idea does some real work. If 83 means 8 ÷ 3, then you can just do the division to find the mixed number. Your book draws it in a little cooking pot, but it is ordinary short division.

1
Set it up: the bottom number goes outside the division bracket, the top number goes inside. For 8/3 that is 3⟌8.
2
Divide: how many 3s fit into 8? Two, because 3 × 2 = 6. Write the 2 on top of the bracket — that is your whole number.
3
Find the remainder: 8 − 6 = 2. That is what is left over after the whole ones have been handed out.
4
Build the answer: the remainder goes on top, the number you divided by stays on the bottom → 23. Put it after the whole number: 223.
8/3 = 8 ÷ 3 = 2 remainder 2 → 2 and 2/3
Say it as a sentence "Eight thirds is 2 whole ones with 2 thirds left over." If you can say that sentence, you have the answer — the writing is just copying the sentence down.

3️⃣ Q3 — whole number or mixed number?

Question 3 mixes in some fractions that divide exactly, with nothing left over. When the remainder is 0 there is no fraction part at all — the answer is a plain whole number, and writing "and 0 somethings" would be silly.

How to spot the exact ones early Before you divide, ask: "does the bottom number go into the top number in my times tables?" 8 and 2 → yes (2 × 4). 27 and 3 → yes (3 × 9). If yes, the answer will be a clean whole number.

⚠️ The three traps in this exercise

Trap 1 — dividing the wrong way round 83 is 8 ÷ 3, never 3 ÷ 8. The big number goes inside the bracket. A good check: an improper fraction is always more than 1, so if your answer comes out smaller than 1, you divided backwards.
Trap 2 — putting the remainder on the bottom For 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3, the answer is 234, not 243. The bottom number never changes — it came from the original fraction and it stays there. Only the remainder is new, and remainders go on top.
Trap 3 — a remainder bigger than the divisor If you write "20 ÷ 3 = 5 remainder 5", stop: 5 is bigger than 3, so another whole 3 still fits. The right answer is 6 remainder 2. The remainder must always be smaller than the number you divided by.
