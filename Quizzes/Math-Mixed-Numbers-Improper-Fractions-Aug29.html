🔑 The one big idea

There are two ways to say the same amount of pie.

Imagine you have 2 whole pies and 3 more quarter-slices. You can describe that in two ways, and both are correct:

Mixed number → 234
You count the whole pies first, then the leftover slices. This is how people talk: "two and three quarters."
Improper fraction → 114
You forget about whole pies and just count every single slice: 4 + 4 + 3 = 11 quarter-slices. The top number is bigger than the bottom — that's what "improper" means.
Remember Nothing changes when you swap between them. It is the same pie, counted a different way. 234 and 114 are equal.
The two rules you will use all day
→
Mixed number into improper fraction: multiply the whole number by the bottom, add the top, keep the same bottom. 234  →  2 × 4 = 8 slices in the whole pies  →  8 + 3 = 11  →  114 Why it works: each whole pie is cut into 4, so 2 whole pies = 8 quarter-slices. Then add the 3 you already had.
←
Improper fraction into mixed number: divide the top by the bottom. The answer is the whole number, the remainder stays on top. 114  →  11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3  →  234 Why it works: you are asking "how many full pies can I build out of 11 slices?" Two full pies (that uses 8), and 3 slices are left over.
Quick check If the top divides exactly, there is no leftover, so the answer is a plain whole number. 123 = 12 ÷ 3 = 4, with nothing left over. So 123 = 4.

The reading trick The word that ends in -ths (fifths, sixths, eighths) is always the bottom number. The number in front of it is the top. "4 fifths" = 4 on top, 5 on the bottom.
Exercise 6, Question 2 — four different-looking questions, one idea
a
The three strings on the number line. Look at the small ticks between 0 and 1: there are 5 equal steps, so each step is 15 of a metre. Now just count steps. String B ends 1 whole metre + 3 more steps  →  135 m String C ends 2 whole metres + 2 more steps  →  225 m Always count the ticks first. The ticks tell you the bottom number, and you cannot read the picture until you know it.
b
The beakers of water. Three beakers are full to the 1-litre line, and the last one is only part full. So the answer is 3 wholes plus that part. Count the marks on the last beaker to find the bottom number, count how many are filled to find the top. 3 full litres + one part-full beaker  →  3 and a bit = a mixed number of litres If the last beaker is marked in fifths and one part is filled, the total is 315 litres.
c
2 + 34 = ? This is the easiest one on the whole page — and it is the whole idea of a mixed number. A mixed number is a whole plus a fraction, just written without the plus sign. 2 + 34 = 234
d
3 − 13 = ? Here you must break one whole open. Take one of the 3 wholes and cut it into thirds — that gives you 33. Now take the third away from that one. 3 = 2 + 33 2 + 33 − 13 = 2 + 23 = 223
Careful A very common slip in (d) is to write 323. But you were taking away, so the answer must be smaller than 3, not bigger. Always ask yourself: "should my answer be bigger or smaller than what I started with?"

Exercise 7, Question 2 — the table with both columns

This one asks for the mixed number and the improper fraction for the same picture. Do it in this order every time, and you cannot go wrong:

1
Look at one shape and count how many equal pieces it is cut into. That number is your bottom, and it never changes.
2
Count the full shapes, then the leftover pieces. Write the mixed number.
3
Turn that mixed number into the improper fraction with the multiply-and-add rule. You already have the answer — you don't need to re-count the picture.
Row (a): pieces per shape = 6 · full shapes = 2 · leftover = 5  →  256  →  2 × 6 + 5 = 17  →  176

📙 Exercise 8 · improper fractions back into mixed numbers

Question 1 — the book's "split it up" method

The book breaks the fraction into two parts: the biggest piece that makes whole shapes, and the leftover.

114 = 84 + 34 = 2 + 34 = 234 185 = 155 + 35 = 3 + 35 = 335

How do you know to split 11 into 8 + 3? You look for the biggest multiple of the bottom number that still fits inside the top. For 114: the multiples of 4 are 4, 8, 12 — and 12 is too big, so you take 8.

The faster way — same answer Just divide. 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3, so the answer is 234. Use the book's splitting method when you have to show your working, and the division shortcut when you just need the answer fast.

📝 Page 76 · the two word problems

Question 4 — Mary and Jim's orange juice

Mary drank 310 litre of orange juice. Jim drank 15 litre less than Mary. How much did they drink altogether?

1
What is the question really asking? "Altogether" means you will add at the end. But you can't add yet — you don't know Jim's amount. So this is a two-step problem.
2
Find Jim first. "1/5 less than Mary" means start at Mary and take 1/5 away. 15 = 210  (re-cut fifths into tenths so both match) Jim = 310 − 210 = 110 litre
3
Now add them together. Together = 310 + 110 = 410 = 25 litre  (divide top and bottom by 2)
This is where you stopped On your worksheet you wrote 310 − 15 and then stopped. That first line is completely right — it finds Jim's juice. You just hadn't answered the question yet, because the question asked for both children together. Whenever a problem says "altogether", "in total" or "in all", your last line must be an addition.
Question 5 — Lily's ribbon

Lily bought 1 yd of ribbon. She used 12 yd to tie a package and 310 yd to make a bow. How much ribbon did she have left?

1
What is the whole? 1 whole yard. Everything comes out of that. Write the whole as a fraction with the bottom number you will need: 1 = 1010
2
Make every piece tenths. 12 = 510  ·  310 stays as it is
3
Take both pieces away. 1010 − 510 − 310 = 210 = 15 yd left
Choosing the bottom number You needed halves and tenths together. Ask: "can the small bottom fit into the big one?" 2 fits into 10 five times — so make everything tenths. You almost never need to multiply the two bottoms together.
