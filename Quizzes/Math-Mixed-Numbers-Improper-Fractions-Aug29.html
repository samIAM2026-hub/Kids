🔑 The big idea

Two ways to say the same amount of pie

You have 2 whole pies and 3 more quarter-slices. There are two ways to say that, and both are right:

Mixed number → 234
Count the whole pies first, then the slices left over. This is how we talk: "two and three quarters."
Improper fraction → 114
Forget whole pies. Count every slice: 4 + 4 + 3 = 11 quarter-slices. The top number is bigger than the bottom — that's what "improper" means.
Remember Nothing changes when you swap between them. It is the same pie, counted a different way. 234 and 114 are equal.
The two rules you need all day
→
Mixed number into improper fraction: times the whole number by the bottom, then add the top. The bottom stays the same. 234  →  2 × 4 = 8 slices in the whole pies  →  8 + 3 = 11  →  114 Each whole pie is cut into 4, so 2 whole pies = 8 quarter-slices. Then add the 3 you already had.
←
Improper fraction into mixed number: divide the top by the bottom. The answer is the whole number, and the remainder stays on top. 114  →  11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3  →  234 You are asking: "how many full pies can I build from 11 slices?" Two full pies (that uses 8 slices), and 3 slices are left over.
One more thing If the top divides exactly, there is nothing left over, so the answer is just a whole number. 123 → 12 ÷ 3 = 4, no remainder. So 123 = 4.

Question 1 — write an improper fraction for each picture

Same pictures, opposite job. This time you do not count whole shapes. You count every equal piece, including the ones inside the full shapes.

The counting trick Never count the pieces one by one — you will lose your place. Do this instead: (number of full shapes × pieces in one shape) + the leftover pieces.

Question 2 — the table with both columns

Each row wants the mixed number and the improper fraction for the same picture. Do it in this order every single time:

1
Look at one shape. Count how many equal pieces it is cut into. That is your bottom number, and it never changes.
2
Count the full shapes, then the leftover pieces. Write the mixed number.
3
Turn that mixed number into the improper fraction with times-and-add. You already have the answer — don't count the picture again.

Question 3 — change each improper fraction

Divide the top by the bottom. The answer is the whole number, the remainder stays on top, and the bottom never changes.

Two things people get wrong 1. When the remainder is 0, write the whole number by itself — 155 is just 3, not 305.
2. The bottom number never changes. If you started in fifths, your leftover is still in fifths.

📝 Page 76 · the two word problems

Question 4 — Mary and Jim's orange juice

Mary drank 310 litre of orange juice. Jim drank 15 litre less than Mary. How much orange juice did they drink altogether?

1
Read it twice and find the last word that matters: "altogether." That means you will add at the end. But you can't add yet, because you don't know Jim's amount. So this question has two steps.
2
Find Jim first. "1/5 less than Mary" means: start at Mary's amount and take 1/5 away. Fifths and tenths are different sizes, so re-cut the fifths into tenths. 15 = 210  (times top and bottom by 2) Jim = 310 − 210 = 110 litre
3
Now add the two children together. Mary + Jim = 310 + 110 = 410 = 25 litre  (divide top and bottom by 2)
This is exactly where you stopped In your book you wrote 310 − 15 and then stopped. That line is right — it finds Jim's juice. You just hadn't answered the question yet, because it asked about both children. When a problem says "altogether", "in total" or "in all", your last line must be a +.
Question 5 — Lily's ribbon

Lily bought 1 yd of ribbon. She used 12 yd to tie a package and 310 yd to make a bow. How much ribbon did she have left?

1
What is the whole? 1 whole yard. Everything comes out of that one yard. But you cannot subtract from a plain "1", so turn it into a fraction first: 1 = 1010 Why tenths? Because the pieces you are taking away are halves and tenths, and 2 fits into 10. Pick the bigger bottom number when the small one fits inside it.
2
Make every piece tenths. 12 = 510  ·  310 is already tenths
3
Take both pieces away, one at a time. 1010 − 510 − 310 = 210 = 15 yd left
Check your answer makes sense She started with 1 yard and used a lot of it, so the answer must be small. 1/5 of a yard is small. If you had got an answer bigger than 1, you would know something went wrong.
