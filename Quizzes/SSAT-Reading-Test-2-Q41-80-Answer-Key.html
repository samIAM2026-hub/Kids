Why each answer is right

Grouped by passage · the proof line from the text is in bold

41–45Dance competitions informational · main idea + inference
41B — to inform people about dance competitions
Purpose questions want the whole passage, not one paragraph. This one walks through sponsoring, practising, judging, scoring and rules — a general explanation of how competitions work. (D) is only the second half of the passage, so it's too narrow.
42D — can be eliminated from competition
Last line: "Routines which fail to comply with the requirements of the competition will be disqualified." Disqualified = eliminated. Nothing is said about their experience or their training.
43C — they help pay for travel to national contests
First line: "Dance schools often participate… by sponsoring teams that go to regional and national competitions." To sponsor is to fund. The schools are never said to provide judges or teach the rulebook.
44Vocab in contextA — appropriately
"Ranks awarded by different judges are combined into a final score, and medals or trophies are awarded accordingly" — i.e. awarded to fit the score. That's "appropriately". Put each choice back in the sentence and only A still makes sense.
45Roman numeralE — I, II and III
All three appear: hours — "average number of hours a week the dancer dances"; group size — "A common limitation is the number of dancers" with categories for solos through super groups; music — "a category which is consistent with the music, style, content" plus "appropriate music". Check numerals one at a time and tick them off in the margin.
46–50The elderly carpenter fable · figurative meaning + moral
46C — one's life
The last paragraph switches from the story to us: "We build our lives in a distracted way… we are now living in the house we have built." The house has stopped being lumber and become a metaphor for a life.
47E — he was about to retire and figured he could relax
He had already announced he was leaving; this last house was "a personal favor". With his mind on retirement he stopped caring, so "sloppy workmanship and substandard materials". He would miss the paycheck — that's regret, not a fear of not being paid, so (C) is a trap.
48B — "Life is a Do-It-Yourself Project"
A title has to cover the whole passage, including the moral. The point is that you build your own life with your own hands, well or badly. The other titles fit only the carpenter's story, not the lesson drawn from it.
49D — he was building his retirement home
"This is your house… my gift to you." Then: "If he had only known he was building his own house, he would have done it all so differently." That's the surprise the whole story turns on.
50A — your life is the result of past attitudes and choices
The closing lines say we react instead of act, settle for less than our best, and then have to live in what we made: "If we had realized, we would have done it differently." That is choices → consequences.
51–55Titan, Saturn's moon science · detail, purpose, prediction
51EXCEPTD — they both have their own moons
Titan is a moon — it orbits Saturn, and nothing orbits it. Everything else is stated: methane rains (rainfall), lakes that "evaporate once more to form the moon's clouds" (evaporation, liquid lakes), and an atmosphere that "behaves in ways similar to Earth's."
52C — describe the findings of a spacecraft mission
That paragraph is a list of what Cassini has done: arrived at Saturn in 2004, "provided information on Saturn's rings, discovered new moons and found an interesting watery geyser on the small moon Enceledus." It's a mission résumé, not an explanation of lake formation.
53D — serves a similar purpose to oceans on Earth
Literally the last sentence: "Methane… in Titan's lakes fills a role played by oceans on our planet." An analogy question is usually answered by the sentence that contains the word "like", "as" or "role of".
54B — it has similar atmospheric processes to Earth
"Titan, whose atmosphere behaves in ways similar to Earth's, is a great scientific opportunity," plus "a chilly model of Earth's climate." Size (D) is mentioned but is never the reason scientists care.
55A — the additional findings of a recent Titan flyby
"What comes next?" questions are answered by the loose thread at the end: "Seventeen more flybys of the moon are planned." The passage sets up more Titan data, so that's what follows.
56–60"Ulalume" — the land of Weir poetry · mood, diction, inference
56C — establish the mood of Weir
Stack the adjectives up: ashen, sober, crisped, sere, withering, lonesome, dim, misty, dank, ghoul-haunted. None of them describe events — they all build one gloomy, haunted atmosphere.
57Vocab in contextD — gray and dry
Ashen = the colour of ash, gray. Sere sits beside "crisped" and "withering", so it must mean dried up. Even if you only know one word, D is the only pair where both halves fit the sentence.
58EXCEPTE — the peacefulness and quiet of a nearby lake
The lake is "the dim lake of Auber" and "the dank tarn of Auber" in a "ghoul-haunted woodland" — eerie, not peaceful. The others are all there: withering leaves (seasons), "lonesome" (loneliness), "It was night" (end of day), "dim… misty" (hard to see far).
59Roman numeralB — I and II only
I yes — the setting is Auber and Weir. II yes — "It was night in the lonesome October." III no — the poem never says why he is going. If the passage doesn't say it, it isn't answered, no matter how reasonable a guess feels.
60A — is drawn to this place of uncertainty and anguish
He calls it "my most immemorial year" and describes the place with lingering, possessive detail — he keeps returning to it in his mind. Nothing suggests a first visit (C), a meeting (B), or eagerness to escape (E).
61–65African-American soldiers in the Civil War history · main idea, tone, implication
61D — the achievement of black soldiers in the Civil War
Every sentence is about what the U.S. Colored Troops accomplished — consistently good fighters, Carney's Medal of Honor, the 54th Massachusetts "created to prove that black men could be good soldiers." (B) fails because no flaws are ever mentioned.
62B — reluctant respect
"Even their enemies had to unwillingly admit that fact." Admitting = respect; unwillingly = reluctant. The adverb is doing all the work — circle words like "unwillingly" when you see them.
63EXCEPTE — what rank did he hold?
Answered: age 23 (A), a USCT member (B), first black soldier to earn the Medal of Honor (C), "with 45 other volunteers" (D). His rank is never given. On EXCEPT questions, tick the four you can find and the leftover is the answer.
64Roman numeralC — I and III only
"Every round fired… gave him a chance to strike a blow against slavery and prove himself equal to his white comrades" — that is III and I in one sentence. Jobs after the war (II) are never mentioned, and the war isn't even over in this passage.
65A — the soldiers in blue uniforms were fighting against slavery
The men in blue fired "at Confederate enemies" to "strike a blow against slavery." Blue = Union = anti-slavery side. Shaw was white (the unit was all-black "with the exception of senior officers"), which kills (B).
66–70Sherlock Holmes on the moor fiction · inference, mood, purpose
66A — a work associate of Sherlock Holmes
"…very hard for those who were acting as his agents and assistants. I had often suffered under it." He puts himself in that group — a colleague, not a relative (Watson).
67EXCEPTE — a drive through a city at a very late hour
They are on the moor — "the dark, empty spaces on either side of the narrow road" — the opposite of a city. All the others are stated: "the cold wind upon our faces" (A), empty open land (B), suffering under Holmes's silence (C), "got down near the gate of the avenue" (D).
68Word in contextC — hatred of revealing his plans ahead of time
The defect is defined in the same sentence: "he was very unwilling to communicate his full plans to any other person until the instant that they happened." It's about timing, not an inability to explain himself, so (B) is the trap.
69D — controlled eagerness
"My nerves thrilled with anticipation" and "our nerves were tense with emotion and anticipation" — eager. But he keeps it in: "that unnatural restraint", polite small talk in front of the driver. Eagerness held on a leash. He softens his complaint too ("if, indeed, one may call it a defect"), so it isn't criticism (A).
70B — so no one would overhear confidential information
"Our conversation was hampered by the presence of the driver of the hired wagonette, so that we were forced to talk of trivial matters." The cause is right there in the sentence — a stranger was listening.
71–75Building the road to Alaska, 1942 history · main idea, reference, inference
71B — history leading up to it being built
The passage runs a timeline: the 1890s gold rushes → a 1930s study → Pearl Harbor → Roosevelt reopening the plans. It stops before construction, so it's the back-story, not the building.
72C — Alaska
"…to connect the United States with the continent's 'far north'." The road's whole purpose is to reach the "remote military outposts in Alaska." The Yukon is what it passes through, not the destination.
73EXCEPTE — the pay was not very good
Money is never mentioned. The recruitment notice covers the rest: mosquitoes and gnats that "cause bodily harm" (A), "swamps, rivers, ice" (B), "90 degrees above zero to 70 degrees below" (C), and "required to work and live under the most extreme conditions" (D).
74A — most of the road did not go through the U.S.
The route runs "over the Canadian Rockies, through the Yukon Territory, all the way to remote military outposts in Alaska" — two long Canadian legs before it reaches U.S. soil. That's an inference the map of the sentence forces.
75D — a demand for gold first sparked interest in the road
"The idea of laying a roadway… can be traced all the way back to the Yukon gold rushes of the 1890s." "Traced back to" = where the interest started. And Roosevelt acted "within a few weeks", so (E) is wrong.
76–80Harold Ickes, "What Is an American?" speech · tone, main idea, figurative language
76EXCEPTB — his status in society
The opening list is what an American is not: "Not his social status nor his bank account." The other four are all on the "is" side — loves justice, fights for his neighbour, sacrifices for his children, fights joyously in a just cause.
77A — blunt
Short, flat, hammering sentences: "Not color nor race nor religion." "Americans are not afraid to fight." No hedging, no argument with an opponent, no sadness — just plain statements driven home.
78D — what does and does not define an American
The passage is literally built in two halves — a list of things that don't count (colour, pedigree, bank account) and a list of things that do (justice, courage, sacrifice). The title question is "What constitutes an American?"
79C — fight with partnering countries against evil
The fire analogy: brutality abroad "would destroy us as surely as a fire raging in our nearby neighbor's house would burn ours if we didn't help to put out his." Help the neighbour or the fire reaches you — join the fight abroad.
80Meaning of a phraseE — America is not free if most of the world is not free
"Inseparable" = they can't be pulled apart. The very next line explains it: "We cannot retain our liberty if three-fourths of the world is enslaved." When a phrase question stumps you, read the sentence right after it — writers usually explain themselves.

Three habits that would pick up most of these points

  1. Point at the proof line. On this test, 41, 42, 43, 49, 53, 62, 64, 70, 73, 75, 76 and 80 are answered almost word-for-word in the passage. Before you fill a bubble, find the sentence. If you can't find one, you're guessing from memory — and memory is where the wrong answers live.
  2. Do EXCEPT questions backwards. There are six of them here (51, 58, 63, 67, 73, 76). Tick off the four choices you can prove, and the one you couldn't tick is the answer. Trying to spot the odd one out by feel is how you lose these.
  3. Roman numerals: judge each numeral alone, then read the choices. For 45, 59 and 64, decide true/false for I, II and III in the margin first, then find the matching letter. Looking at the answer choices too early makes tempting combinations look right.
And the cheapest points of all: no blanks. A guess with one option crossed out is worth money on average. Cross out, commit, move on — the clock is the other opponent.
