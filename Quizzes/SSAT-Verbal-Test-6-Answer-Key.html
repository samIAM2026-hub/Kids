The 9 misses, explained

These are the ones where a circle went on the wrong letter

3 · ALOOFchose E · alienanswer: A · reserved
This one hurts, because the sentence written underneath was perfect: "That person is quite unapproachable because of her aloof attitude." Unapproachable = keeping your distance = reserved. Alien means foreign or from somewhere else — an aloof person is standing right next to you, they are just cool and distant. The lesson: after writing the sentence, go back and pick the option that matches the sentence. The knowledge was there; the circle just landed on the wrong word.
18 · PREROGATIVEchose A · commandanswer: B · choice
A prerogative is a special right that you may use if you want to — "It's a lady's prerogative to change her mind." The key word is may: it is a privilege, not an order. A command is something you must obey. The sentence written on the paper ("the commander has the prerogative to abuse his soldiers") shows the word was read as "power" — close, but the exact meaning is the freedom to choose.
24 · CAUCUSchose E · cosmeticanswer: C · meeting
This looks like a pure sound-alike slip — caucus next to cosmetic, both starting with a hard C. A caucus is a meeting of a group of people from the same political party to plan or vote ("the Iowa caucus"). Nothing to do with make-up. Rule: if you don't know the word, never pick the option that just sounds like it. That is the exact answer the test wants you to grab.
27 · AMULETchose B · necklaceanswer: C · charm
A genuinely close call, and the reasoning was good — amulets often are worn on a necklace. But the definition is an object worn because it is believed to give protection or luck. That is a charm. A necklace is just jewellery; an amulet could equally be a ring, a stone in a pocket, or a bracelet. Test it this way: "Every amulet is a necklace" — false. "Every amulet is a charm" — true. The answer is the one that is true every time.
29 · WREAKchose A · smashanswer: E · inflict
Think of the only phrase this word ever appears in: "wreak havoc." You wreak havoc, wreak destruction, wreak revenge — you bring it down onto something. That is inflict. Smash is too physical and too narrow: you can wreak havoc on a schedule or on someone's plans, and nothing gets physically smashed.
35 · Earth : satellitechose B · Earth : Sunanswer: A · nucleus : electron
Say the sentence first: "A satellite orbits around the Earth." So the answer needs centre-first, orbiter-second. An electron orbits around a nucleus — same shape, same order. "Earth is to Sun" is the trap because it uses the same word: but here the Earth is the one doing the orbiting, so the pair is backwards. Direction matters as much as meaning.
36 · Composer : musicchose A · actor : playanswer: C · sculptor : statue
"A composer creates music." Does an actor create a play? No — a playwright writes the play; the actor only performs one that already exists. A sculptor creates a statue: same verb, exactly. Be strict with the verb in your sentence. "Has something to do with" is not a relationship; "creates" is.
42 · Tornado : windchose D · famine : droughtanswer: B · monsoon : rain
"A tornado is an extreme, violent form of wind." A monsoon is an extreme, violent form of rain — identical. Famine and drought is a cause and effect pair: a drought causes a famine; a famine is not "extreme drought." Both options are about weather disasters, which is why D felt right — but the topic being similar is not the relationship.
46 · Flowing : streamchose E · tidal : waveanswer: C · stagnant : pond
"Flowing is the natural state of a stream — that is what a stream always does." Stagnant (still, not moving) is the natural state of a pond. It even makes a neat opposite pair with the question, which is a good sign. Tidal : wave fails because waves are not caused by tides — they are made by wind — and "tidal" is not what a wave permanently is.
52 · Ore : minechose C · wood : tree ✱answer: A · granite : quarry
Half a mark for this one. "Ore is the raw material dug out of a mine." Granite is the raw material dug out of a quarry — a mine and a quarry are both man-made extraction sites, so the match is exact. Wood does come from a tree, but a tree is a living thing, not a place you dig. When two options both "work," pick the one that matches more features of the original.

The 16 blanks, answered

Nothing here is beyond you — several are easy points that were simply never attempted

28 · SUCCUMBleft blankanswer: A · yield
To succumb is to stop resisting and give in — "he succumbed to temptation," "she succumbed to the illness." That is yield. Watch out for (D) succeed: it looks similar and starts with the same three letters, but it means the opposite of giving up.
32 · Plausible : definiteleft blankanswer: E · probable : certain
Plausible = it might be true. Definite = it definitely is. So the relationship is same idea, weaker version → stronger version. Probable (likely) → certain (sure) is exactly that step up in strength. (C) correct : accurate fails because those two are equally strong.
37 · Dearth : surplusleft blankanswer: C · dreary : happy
A dearth is a shortage — not enough. A surplus is too much. Straight opposites, so the answer must be two opposites: dreary (gloomy) and happy. Worth memorising: dearth shows up on the SSAT constantly and it is easy to mistake for "death."
40 · Debility : strengthleft blankanswer: A · modesty : arrogance
Debility is weakness — it is the absence of strength. Modesty is the absence of arrogance. Same word family as debilitating (something that makes you weak), which is the clue if the noun looks unfamiliar.
41 · Vehemence : serenityleft blankanswer: B · solace : sorrow
Vehemence is fierce, forceful passion; serenity is total calm — opposite feelings. Solace (comfort) and sorrow (grief) are also opposite feelings. (D) adversity : happiness is tempting, but adversity is a situation (hard times), not a feeling, so the word types don't match.
43 · Gregarious : socialleft blankanswer: E · introspective : withdrawn
Gregarious means loving to be in a crowd — a strong version of social. So look for another pair where the first word is an intense version of the second and both point the same way. Introspective (thinking deeply inward) and withdrawn both mean turned away from other people. Note that (C) and (D) are both opposite-pairs, so they are out immediately.
44 · Waist : manleft blankanswer: D · equator : world
Say it plainly: "The waist is the line around the middle of a man." The equator is the line around the middle of the world. This is a lovely question once you say the sentence out loud — and impossible if you only stare at the words.
45 · Probing : deepleft blankanswer: A · superficial : surface
Probing means going deep into something (a probing question digs). So: "probing means going deep." Superficial means staying on the surface. The two pairs are even mirror images of each other, which confirms it.
48 · Advisor : counselorleft blankanswer: A · mentor : professor
An advisor and a counselor are both people who guide you — near-synonyms, two names for roughly the same role. A mentor and a professor are likewise both people who teach and guide. Every other option is a different relationship: child/parent and tooth/dentist are opposites or services, learning/teacher is an activity and a person.
51 · Episodic : constantleft blankanswer: B · sporadic : steady
Episodic = happening in separate bursts, on and off (think: episodes of a show). Constant = never stopping. Opposites. Sporadic (scattered, now and then) versus steady is the same opposition. (A), (C), (D) and (E) are all pairs of synonyms, so all four are wrong for the same reason — a nice example of eliminating four choices in one move.
54 · Splicing : splitting :: fusion :left blankanswer: B · fission
Splicing means joining two things together; splitting is pulling them apart — opposites. Fusion is joining (the Sun fuses atoms together), so the opposite is fission — splitting an atom. The other four options are look-alike f-words planted to confuse: fizzle, flatten, fissure, foster.
56 · Comma : pauseleft blankanswer: C · flag : country
"A comma is a symbol that stands for a pause." A flag is a symbol that stands for a country. Nothing else on the list is a symbol-for-a-thing: state/union and cloth/uniform are part-to-whole, and president/coin is nonsense.
57 · Refrigerator : meat :: bank :left blankanswer: D · money
"A refrigerator is where you store meat to keep it safe." A bank is where you store money to keep it safe. This is one of the easiest questions on the whole page — strong evidence that the blanks at the end are a timing problem, not a knowledge problem.
58 · Gymnasium : game :: auditorium :left blankanswer: A · production
"A gymnasium is the place where a game happens." An auditorium is the place where a production (a play, a concert) happens. Actors and director are people, and script is an object — only "production" is the event that fills the room.
59 · Deft : awkwardleft blankanswer: B · skillful : clumsy
Deft means quick and skilful with your hands — the opposite of awkward. So: skillful and clumsy. Three of the other options (quick/swift, smooth/slick, rustic/remote) are synonym-ish pairs, which rules them out the moment you notice the question is an opposite pair.
60 · Despise : esteemleft blankanswer: D · enemies : friends
To despise someone is to look down on them with contempt; to esteem them is to look up to them with respect. Opposite attitudes toward people — which is exactly enemies versus friends. (B) hate : dislike is the trap: those point the same way, just at different strengths.

The sentences underneath — four to fix

Writing your own sentence is a great habit. These four show the meaning wasn't quite locked in.

WordWhat was writtenWhy it doesn't work · a better sentence
FOIBLE"That kid is quite foible…" Foible is a noun, not an adjective — a small, harmless weakness in someone's character. You can't be foible. → "Talking to his houseplants is one of his foibles."
PROMINENT"Warren made the prominent decision to study hard." Prominent means standing out so people notice it — famous, or physically sticking out. A private decision isn't prominent. → "She is a prominent scientist," or "a prominent nose."
RATIFY"Upon being ratified to enter the contest…" You ratify a document or agreement, not a person. → "The senate ratified the treaty." For a person, the word you wanted is approved or accepted.
PREROGATIVE"That commander has the prerogative to abuse his soldiers." A prerogative is a right you may choose to use, not permission to do harm. → "Setting the training schedule is the commander's prerogative."
Everything else was used correctly — chide, innocuous, veracious, deter, hostile, abdicate, boundary, illiterate, perilous, stationary, proficient, agile, ambiguous, intriguing, clandestine, bounteous, benign. The Elsa/Anna sentence for abdicate is genuinely the best way to remember that word.

Traps to remember

  1. Finish the paper. Questions 56–60 were left completely empty, and 57 ("a bank stores money") is one of the easiest on the page. That is roughly 5 free points lost to the clock. On the analogies, move fast and come back — don't spend two minutes on one hard pair while five easy ones go unread.
  2. Say the relationship in a full sentence before looking at the choices. "A composer creates music." "A satellite orbits the Earth." Then try that exact sentence on all five options. Questions 35, 36, 42, 44 and 56 all fall over instantly once the sentence is said out loud.
  3. Check the direction. #35 was lost purely to direction — Earth : satellite is centre-then-orbiter, so "Earth is to Sun" is backwards. If the pair is flipped, it is wrong no matter how good the words look.
  4. Same topic ≠ same relationship. #42 chose famine : drought because it was also about weather disasters. Weather was never the relationship — "an extreme version of" was.
  5. Read your own sentence before you circle. #3 (aloof) had a perfect sentence written right next to a wrong circle. One five-second check would have caught it.
  6. Never pick the sound-alike. caucus ≠ cosmetic (#24), succumb ≠ succeed (#28). The option that merely sounds like the word is the trap, every single time.
  7. Guess when you can eliminate two. A wrong answer costs ¼ point; a blank costs a whole one. On #51, four of the five options were synonym pairs when the question was an opposite pair — that is a guaranteed point available with no vocabulary at all.
About the ✱ question. #52 (ore : mine) is the one place the book is soft. Both granite : quarry and wood : tree describe a raw material and its source; granite : quarry wins only because a quarry, like a mine, is a place people dig. It is a fair miss — don't lose sleep over it.
