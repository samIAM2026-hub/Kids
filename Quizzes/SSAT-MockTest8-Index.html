One section at a time

The whole paper, split into six timed sittings. Each one is a real test: no answers while you work, then a score and a full explanation for every question.

Why it is split this way

The real SSAT runs about 1 minute per math question, 1 minute per reading question and 30 seconds per verbal question. Every sitting below keeps that pace, so the practice is honest — it is only the sitting length that is shorter. The reading section breaks between two passages, so no passage is ever split across two sittings. Do one a day, or two on a long afternoon; the scores add up to a whole paper either way.

About the answer key. This copy of the paper came without its key, so every answer here was worked out from scratch: all 50 maths questions were computed and then checked a second way, and each verbal and reading answer was argued from the passage. Five answers are judgement calls rather than certainties, and each one says so in a box under its explanation:
  • Reading A, Q4 — the writer’s purpose: a little history of dentistry over answering frequently asked questions.
  • Reading A, Q5 — which problem dentists cannot fix: neither root canals nor bad breath is named in the passage; root canals is set as the answer.
  • Reading A, Q6 — the best title: Unexpected Discoveries over the pun A Thousand and One Words.
  • Analogies, Q43 — greet is to salute: read as general-to-specific, which makes dress is to evening gown the match.
  • Analogies, Q53 — glorious is to exalted: two synonym pairs are offered; trivial is to unimportant is the exact one.
Two printing quirks in the original have been left exactly as they appear rather than tidied up: Analogies Q49 lists sound twice, as (B) and (E); and Reading A Q9 refers to “line 12” when the one-word paragraph it means is line 15. Learning to work around a paper’s own slips is part of the practice. If the printed key turns up and disagrees on any of these, the page is the thing to change — not the reasoning in it.
