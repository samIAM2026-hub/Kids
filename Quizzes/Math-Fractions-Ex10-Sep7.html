1️⃣ Q1 — improper fraction → whole or mixed number

What the question is really asking

An improper fraction is a fraction whose top number is bigger than (or equal to) its bottom number, like 124. It is a perfectly good number — it is just wearing an untidy coat. Question 1 asks you to tidy it up: say how many whole ones it makes, and what is left over.

The recipe — divide, then dress the leftovers

1
Divide the top number by the bottom number. The answer to the division is your whole number.
2
Look at the remainder. If there is nothing left over, you are finished — the answer is just a whole number.
3
Put the remainder on top of the same bottom number. That is the fraction part.
4
Simplest form: see if the fraction part can be made smaller by dividing its top and bottom by the same number.
25 ÷ 10 = 2 remainder 5  →  2 and 5/10  →  divide top and bottom by 5  →  2 and 1/2
What "simplest form" means A fraction is in its simplest form when no number except 1 divides into the top and the bottom. 510 is not simplest — 5 goes into both. 12 is simplest — nothing goes into 1 except 1. If you are not sure, try dividing by 2, then 3, then 5. If none of them work on both numbers, you are done.
⚠️ The two mistakes here 1. Stopping before you simplify. 2510 is the right amount, but the question asked for simplest form, so it is still marked wrong. Always ask "can the leftover fraction shrink?"
2. Putting the remainder on the bottom. The bottom number never changes — it is the size of the slices. Only the leftover count goes on top.

2️⃣ Q2 — join each pair of equal numbers

This is the animal-trapping puzzle. All the numbers look like mixed numbers, but look closely: some of them have a fraction part that is improper — the top is bigger than the bottom, like 232. Those are the sneaky ones, and each of them is equal to a tidy mixed number somewhere else on the page.

Why 232 is really 312

Read it as "2 wholes and 3 halves". But 3 halves is more than a whole — 2 of those halves make another whole one, with 1 half left over. So:

2 + 3/2  =  2 + 1 + 1/2  =  3 and 1/2
1
Look at the fraction part only. Is the top bigger than the bottom?
2
If yes, tidy it up the same way you did in Q1: divide top by bottom, and add the whole ones you get to the whole number already sitting in front.
3
Now hunt the other row for that tidy answer, and draw the line.

The free shortcut A pair can only ever join if the bottom numbers match. So before you calculate anything, look at the bottom number and cross out every partner that does not have the same one. Usually that leaves you with only one or two to check.

3️⃣ Q3 — adding, answer in simplest form

Case A — the bottom numbers are already the same

If both fractions are made of the same size slices, adding is easy: add the tops, keep the bottom. Two fifths plus three fifths is five fifths — you are just counting slices.

3/5 + 2/5 = 5/5 = 1   ·   4/7 + 5/7 = 9/7 = 1 and 2/7

Then tidy the answer, exactly like Q1: if the top ends up equal to the bottom it is 1 whole; if it is bigger, change it to a mixed number; and simplify whatever is left.

⚠️ Never add the bottom numbers 3/5 + 2/5 is not 5/10. Think about it: 5/10 is a half, but three fifths on its own is already more than a half. The bottom number is not a number of things — it is the name of the slice size. Adding "fifths + fifths" gives you fifths, not tenths.
Case B — the bottom numbers are different

You cannot add halves to sixths any more than you can add cats to bicycles. First you must make them the same kind of slice. In every question in your book, one bottom number goes into the other one exactly, so you only have to change one fraction.

1
Ask: does the small bottom number go into the big one? For 1/2 + 5/6 — yes, 2 goes into 6 three times.
2
Times the top and the bottom by the same number. 1/2 → times both by 3 → 3/6. It is the same amount, cut into more pieces.
3
Now both are sixths, so add the tops: 3/6 + 5/6 = 8/6.
4
Tidy: 8/6 = 1 and 2/6 = 1 and 1/3.
Why timesing top and bottom is allowed Because you are multiplying by 1 in disguise. 3⁄3 is 1, and multiplying by 1 changes nothing. Cutting each half into 3 pieces gives you more pieces, but exactly the same amount of pizza. This is the single most useful move in the whole fractions unit — it is how you will add any two fractions later on.

4️⃣ Q4 — taking a fraction away from a whole number

1 − 79 looks strange because there is no fraction to take the sevenths from. The fix is to borrow one whole one and cut it into slices the same size as the fraction you are subtracting.

1
Look at the bottom number of the fraction you are taking away. Here it is 9.
2
Turn one whole into that many slices. One whole = 99. (One whole is always "the bottom number over itself".)
3
Subtract: 9/9 − 7/9 = 2/9. Tops take away, bottom stays.

If you start with more than one whole, only one of them gets cut up — the rest stay whole and just wait at the front of the answer:

2 − 3/4  =  1 + 4/4 − 3/4  =  1 + 1/4  =  1 and 1/4 3 − 4/7  =  2 + 7/7 − 4/7  =  2 + 3/7  =  2 and 3/7
⚠️ Check your answer is smaller You took something away, so the answer must be less than what you started with. If you work out 3 − 4/7 and get 3 3/7, something has gone wrong — you have more than you began with. And notice the pattern: the whole number in the answer is always one less than the one you started with, because one whole got chopped up.

5️⃣ Q5 — write >, < or = in the circle

The sign is a hungry mouth: it always opens towards the bigger number. > means "bigger than", < means "smaller than", = means "exactly the same".

The trick is that the two numbers are usually wearing different coats — one mixed number and one improper fraction. So change them into the same coat first, then compare. Use whichever direction is less work.

Three quick checks, in this order

1
Is one of them less than 1? A fraction with a smaller top than bottom is under 1. Anything with a whole number in front is over 1. That settles it instantly — 67 < 1.
2
Are the whole-number parts different? Change the improper fraction to a mixed number and compare the front numbers. 113 is 3 and a bit, and 1011 is under 1 — so 10/11 < 11/3.
3
Same whole number? Then and only then compare the fraction parts. 103 = 3 and 1/3, and the other number is 3 and 1/3 — so the answer is =.
Watch out for the "=" ones Three of the ten in your book are equals, and they are easy to miss because the two sides look completely different. 125 and 225 are the same number in two coats. Whenever the bottom numbers match, check for = before anything else.
