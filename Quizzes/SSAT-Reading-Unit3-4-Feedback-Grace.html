He jumped.

1. “The other kids were a row of wet birds along the far wall.” This is — ✗ wrong

Comment

The whole test is one word: is “like” or “as” in the sentence?

Look again. The other kids were a row of wet birds. There is no “like.” There is no “as.” The sentence does not say the kids were similar to birds — it says they were birds. When a writer says one thing simply is another thing, that is a metaphor.

Why you fell for it: the sentence feels like a comparison, and “simile” is the word we say first when we talk about comparisons. But feeling like a comparison is not the test. Both similes and metaphors are comparisons. The only thing separating them is whether the writer used the word like or as.
The rule, in nine words
Simile says like. Metaphor says is.

Simile: “Her whistle hung around her neck like a small silver threat.”
Metaphor: “The deep end was a room with no floor.”
Same job, different tool. Point your finger at the word like — if your finger lands on nothing, it is a metaphor.

Why the hard part is done. A simile is not invented out of nowhere. It grows out of a body feeling — and you already wrote three of them down. Palms sweating. Throat tight. Legs shaking. Every one of those is a simile with the comparison missing. You brought the ingredients and forgot to cook.

Here is the method. Three steps, and you never get stuck again.

  1. Name the feeling exactly. Not “I was nervous.” What was your body doing? My legs were shaking.
  2. Ask: what else in the world does that? Say four things out loud, fast, even silly ones. Jelly. A washing machine. A phone buzzing on a table. A cold little dog.
