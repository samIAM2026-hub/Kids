Why each answer is right

Grouped by passage · the proof line from the text is in bold

1–5Native Americans history · purpose, tone, detail
1C — give a brief history of Native Americans
The passage marches through time: arrival from Asia 20,000 years ago → organized societies → fighting the European settlers → Geronimo → reservations. That's a short history. (E) is the trap — tribes are named, but none of their separate histories is told.
2C — to become famous throughout the country
"Although he eventually was captured, he later became a celebrity." Celebrity = famous. The word "although" tells you the sentence is about to swing from bad news to good, so disgrace (A) and dying alone (D) are out.
3B — objective
Read the sentences again: they state facts and take no side. No angry words, no admiring words, no "sadly" or "unfairly". On tone questions, if you can't find an emotional word in the passage, the answer is almost always the neutral choice.
4B — the life of the Native American in modern society
The passage ends on reservations "where many still reside today." The word "today" leaves the reader in the present, so the natural next paragraph is modern life. "What comes next?" is always answered by the last sentence.
5EXCEPTD — gathering
"…many were skilled hunters, farmers, and fishers" covers hunting, farming and fishing; fighting shows up in the raids and rebellions. Gathering never appears. It sounds like something the passage would say — that's exactly why it's the answer.
6–11Deserts science · main idea, inference, vocab
6A — deserts and desert wildlife
Paragraph 1 is deserts, paragraphs 2 and 3 are the animals in them. The other choices (nocturnal animals, plants, water sources, temperatures) are each one detail inside the passage, not the whole of it.
7C — the long ears of the hedgehog that give off heat to cool the animal
The passage defines desert adaptation as coping with water and extreme temperatures — camels going days without drinking, thick fur, hunting at night. Only the hedgehog's ears solve a temperature problem. Claws, shells, hoods and speed are about food and predators, which any animal anywhere needs.
8B — a general book on desert life
It's written to a beginner — "It may seem that…", "When most people think of deserts" — with no data, no citations, no technical terms. Too simple for a thesis (A) or an advanced text (C), and it has no personal voice, so not a diary (D) or biography (E).
9E — they can store water for many days
"Camels can survive for days without drinking." That is the only thing the passage says about camels. Thick fur is mentioned, but for "many of the mammals" and to keep heat and cold out — not for camels specifically.
10D — can be in a cold climate
"…some, like those at high altitudes, may actually be quite cold." The whole first paragraph exists to break the "deserts = hot sand" assumption. Lush plants, lakes and heavy rainfall would all contradict what a desert is.
11Vocab in contextB — harsh
The sentence explains itself with a dash: "the desert is a punishing place — it is difficult to find food and water." Difficult, not lethal, so "deadly" is too strong. And a place can't literally hurt or be unhappy — punishing here means tough on whatever lives there.
12–16The Olympic Games informational · main idea, tone, inference
12D — comparing the modern Olympic Games to those in Ancient Greece
Paragraph 1 is the ancient games, paragraph 2 opens "Today's Olympic Games are quite a bit different", and paragraph 3 ties them together. Then-versus-now is the structure of the whole passage. (B) covers only the first paragraph, (C) only part of the second.
13A — give an example of how the opening ceremony is so spectacular
Same sentence: "The opening ceremony is a spectacular display, usually incorporating the traditional dances and culture of the host city." The dances are the evidence for "spectacular". When a detail sits right after a big claim, its job is to prove that claim.
14B — upbeat
"Spectacular display", "the highlight", "the spirit of competition is still alive." Positive and enthusiastic throughout. Note that four of the five choices are negative or flat — when the passage clearly likes its subject, the odd positive choice is usually the answer.
15B — the spirit of Ancient Greek competition
The last three lines spell it out: the torch is carried "from Olympia, the site of the ancient Greek games", and "the spirit of competition is still alive. The flame represents that spirit." Both halves — ancient Greece and competition — have to be in the answer.
16C — the Olympics used to be held in just one country
"The Olympics are no longer held only in one country." "No longer" is a time word: it tells you that they once were. (D) is a trap — the passage says "skiing to gymnastics" and never mentions ice skating at all.
17–21Turtles science · purpose, contrast, tone
17A — illustrate the age of the turtle fossils
"The earliest fossils… are about 200 million years old and date from the time when dinosaurs roamed Earth." The dinosaurs are there as a yardstick — a number that big means nothing until you attach it to something a reader can picture. Turtles are never said to be prey (D).
18C — live in different climates
The question asks how turtles differ from other reptiles, so hunt for the contrast word: "In contrast to other reptiles, whose populations are confined largely to the tropics, turtles are most abundant in southeastern North America and southeastern Asia." They also live in lakes, marshes, forests and deserts. (A) is true of turtles but isn't a difference.
19E — skeptical
"…but reports of turtles living more than a century are questionable." Questionable = he doubts it. Skeptical is doubt; ridiculing (B) would mean he mocks the idea, which is much harsher than one careful word.
20B — count the rings on its shell
Last sentence: "The ages of some turtles can be estimated by counting the growth rings that form each year on the external bony plates of the shell." One ring per year, like a tree.
21B — there is a lot to be learned about turtles
The author calls the century-old reports "questionable", says ages "can be estimated", and only "some" turtles at that — a writer flagging the gaps in what we know. (C) overshoots: the passage says turtles live longer than most other animals, not all.
22–25Summer holidays in Norway (Roald Dahl) memoir · purpose, vocab, analogy
22A — his affection for Norway
"Those magic words!", "shivers of joy rippling over my skin", "idyllic" twice, and going there "was like going home." Every sentence is warm. The half-siblings and the four-day journey are details inside that feeling, not the point of it.
23Vocab in contextB — pleasant
It sits in a sentence about joy and magic: "All my summer holidays… were idyllic." Even without knowing the word, the surrounding mood forces a positive meaning, and "pleasant" is the only positive choice on the list.
24E — how happy and comfortable he was there
Why compare a place to home? Because home means belonging and ease — his family was Norwegian, they spoke the language, all their relations were there. The comparison is about feeling, not about frequency (B) or duration (D).
25B — make the reader understand why the trip was an adventure
He introduces it with "Even the journey was an event" and then explains: no commercial aeroplanes, four days each way. "Event" is positive, so boredom (C) and pity (A) contradict his own framing.
26–31Seashells and shellfish science · figurative meaning, title, contrast
26Word in contextD — the shell
"…do you ever think about whose home that shell was before you found it?" The sentence names it directly — the shell is the home. The next line confirms it: "seashells are the home of a whole group of creatures known as shellfish."
27E — how do shellfish make their shells?
Paragraph 2 answers exactly that: "They draw calcium carbonate, a mineral, from the water… to build the shell up layer by layer." Reproduction, weight and life span are never touched; feeding is mentioned only as what the hinge opens for, never what they eat.
28D — a general discussion of shells
Three paragraphs: whose shells they are, how they're made, and the two kinds. That's a broad tour of shells. (E) is too narrow (only paragraph 2), and habitats (C) are never discussed at all.
29A — illustrate how the shell opens and closes
The comparison is finished by the passage itself: "the two pieces are hinged together, like a door, so that the shell can open and close for feeding." An analogy question is usually answered by the rest of the same sentence.
30C — "I Can Grow My Own Home!"
A good title covers the whole passage. This one catches both big ideas at once: the shell is a home, and the animal makes it itself — "It may surprise you to learn that the shellfish themselves make the shells." The others are about beaches or beauty, which the passage only mentions in its opening line.
31E — the conch shell has fewer pieces than the clam shell
"There are those that are a single unit, like a conch's shell, and those that are in two pieces, like a clam's shell." One piece versus two. Value, beauty and protection are never compared — those choices are pure invention.
32–35"The Bat" — Theodore Roethke poetry · imagery, figurative language
32C — the bat's wings
"His fingers make a hat about his head." A bat's wings are its long finger bones — folded up around its head while it hangs in the attic, they look like a hat. The word "hat" is a picture of the wings, not of the head itself.
33Roman numeralD — I and III only
I yes — "cousin to the mouse" and "mice with wings". III yes — "loops in crazy figures half the night." II no — a vampire is never mentioned. Bats make you think of vampires, and the test is counting on that; judge each numeral only by what is printed on the page.
34B — the pattern of the bat's flight
"He loops in crazy figures half the night / Among the trees." "Loops" and "figures" are shapes drawn in the air — the flight path. The trees are where he does it, not what the figures are.
35C — bats are feared by many
The last three lines: "We are afraid of what our eyes have seen: / For something is amiss or out of place / When mice with wings can wear a human face." The poem ends on fear, and "we" means people in general. (B) is a trap — "cousin to the mouse" is a poetic image, not a claim about biology.
36–40Sports uniforms informational · purpose, attitude, detail
36E — give some reasons why uniforms are useful
"Uniforms are important for many different reasons, whether you are playing a sport or watching one." That's the thesis, and the two paragraphs after it are the reasons — players first, then fans. (B) is only one reason out of several, so it's too narrow.
37Vocab in contextC — endorsement
"You can show support for the team you favor by wearing the colors." That's backing a team publicly — endorsement. The trap here is that "support" also means food, money, or a brace; put each meaning back in the sentence and only endorsement survives.
38B — they have many different purposes
The passage lists safety, speed, ease of movement, protection, grip, telling players apart, and showing team loyalty. That is variety, which also kills (A) — "You may notice how different uniforms are for different sports." (C) picks just one purpose.
39D — give them freedom of movement
"If you participate in track and field, your uniform is designed to help you run faster and move more easily." Protection (C) is the answer to a different sentence — that one is about boxing and football. Match the choice to the right sport.
40A — they help them to distinguish teams
"Imagine how difficult it would be to tell the players on a field apart without their uniforms." That's the first and main reason given for watchers. Colours are mentioned, but for showing loyalty — souvenirs, collecting and price never come up at all.

Three habits worth the most points on this test

  1. Find the proof sentence before you bubble. On this section, 2, 9, 10, 15, 20, 26, 29, 31, 39 and 40 are answered nearly word-for-word in the passage. If you can't point at a line, you're answering from memory — and memory is where the wrong answers hide.
  2. Watch for the signal words. "In contrast to" gave you 18, "no longer" gave you 16, "questionable" gave you 19, "although" gave you 2. Circle contrast and time words as you read; the test builds questions on them.
  3. Beware the answer that sounds like the topic. Gathering (5), vampires (33), ice skating (16) and "bats are related to mice" (35) all feel right for the subject but appear nowhere in the text. Right-sounding is not the same as written down.
Pacing: 40 questions in 40 minutes over 8 passages ≈ 5 minutes per passage, reading included. If a single question eats more than a minute, mark it, guess with one choice crossed out, and come back — one hard question is never worth two easy ones.
