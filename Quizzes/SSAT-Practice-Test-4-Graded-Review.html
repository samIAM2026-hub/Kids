Section 2 · Math — what went wrong

Every miss and the blank, worked through step by step

Math 42 cups of kibble, twice a day · 2 weeksmarked B (28)answer: D (56)
The trap word is twice. Two cups twice a day is 4 cups a day, not 2. Two weeks = 14 days, so 4 × 14 = 56. Marking 28 means only 2 × 14 was done — the second feeding got dropped. When a question gives a rate and a frequency, find the daily total first, then multiply by the days.
Math 5Perimeter of the rectangle (2,4) (2,9) (8,4) (8,9)left blankanswer: D (22)
Don't be scared off by coordinates — just look at the numbers. The x-values are only 2 and 8, so the rectangle is 8 − 2 = 6 wide. The y-values are only 4 and 9, so it is 9 − 4 = 5 tall. Perimeter = 2(6 + 5) = 22. A quick sketch on the page takes ten seconds and makes this a giveaway.
Math 8Next number in 3, 5, 9, 17, ___marked B (34)answer: A (33)
Write the gaps underneath: 3 →5 is +2, 5→9 is +4, 9→17 is +8. The gaps double, so the next gap is +16: 17 + 16 = 33. Choosing 34 is the "double it" guess (17 × 2), which the test puts in on purpose. Always write the differences down — sequences are decided by the gaps, not by the numbers.
Math 13Which item's sales rose by 50% from original to sale price?marked C (Cheese)answer: A (Cereal)
A 50% increase means the new bar is 1.5 times the old one. Cereal goes 80 → 120, and 80 × 1.5 = 120. ✓ Cheese goes 60 → 120 — that is double, a 100% increase. Cheese is tempting because 120 is the tallest bar, but the question asks about the change, not the height.
Math 16Club grew from 48 members to 64 — percent increase?marked D (50%)answer: C (33%)
Percent increase = change ÷ original. The change is 64 − 48 = 16. 16 ÷ 48 = 0.333… = 33%. Marking 50% comes from comparing 16 to something else (or from 48 → 72 thinking). The bottom of the fraction is always the number you started with.
Math 18Which set is in order from least to greatest?marked Danswer: A
Turn every fraction into a decimal first: 4/7 = 0.571, 5/8 = 0.625. Now line them up: 0.571 < 0.6 < 0.625 < 0.7, which is 4/7, 0.6, 5/8, 0.7 — choice (A). The scratch work on the page (4/7, 6/10, 5/8) shows the right method; 0.6 just got put in front of 4/7. 4/7 is a bit more than a half but less than 0.6.
Math 23Solve 16 − (−4y) = −28marked C (−3)answer: A (−11)
Subtracting a negative adds: 16 − (−4y) becomes 16 + 4y. So 16 + 4y = −28 → 4y = −44 → y = −11. Rewrite the double-negative before you start solving — that one rewrite is the whole question.
Pattern in the math misses: not one of these is a topic you don't know — they are all a skipped step. "Twice a day" (Q4), the doubling gap (Q8), dividing by the original (Q16), the double negative (Q23). Slow down for two seconds on the second-to-last step. That alone would have made this a 23 or 24 out of 25.

Every miss, with the line from the passage that decides it

Reading 1The Railway Children · main concernmarked Danswer: B
Follow the last paragraph: "it was just like that with the sorrow the children had felt. It made a deep impression, but the impression did not last long." Everything before it — the cold platform, the dough — is building toward that. So the passage is about how the children experienced sadness. Curiosity is only the vehicle for the dough picture.
Reading 2What the author suggests about the railwaymarked Banswer: D
The closing sentence: the hills, the canal and "above all, the railway, were so new and so perfectly pleasing that the remembrance of the old life… grew to seem almost like a dream." New things crowding out the sad old ones = distracted them from their sadness. "Dream of better futures" is never said.
Reading 3"crock" (line 15) most nearly meansmarked Aanswer: C
A crock is an earthenware pot — a container. Test it by substitution: "the great container of dough set by the fire to rise" works; a pile of dough isn't something you set by the fire. When a word is unknown, put each choice back in the sentence.
Reading 6The author does all of the following EXCEPTmarked Canswer: A
(C) is something he clearly does — "Have you ever gone into a farmhouse kitchen…" is second person. The dough memory belongs to the reader, not to Peter, Roberta and Phyllis, so the author never recounts the children's interest in dough. On EXCEPT questions, tick off the four you can point to in the text; whatever is left is the answer.
Reading 8"maligned" (line 1) most likely meansmarked Banswer: D
Read the whole sentence: "Although crows are much maligned for being noisy or destructive, many people find them engaging and delightful." "Although" signals a contrast with "delightful", so the missing word must be negative. Maligned = spoken badly of = disparaged. "Discussed" is neutral, so it can't carry the contrast.
Reading 9Purpose of the questions in lines 15–19marked Eanswer: C
There are three questions there, and each one is answered in the very next sentence. That's a writer's device for setting up explanations about crow gift-giving. Choice (E) describes only the first question — too narrow to cover all three.
Reading 10Most crucial to scientific understandingmarked Danswer: B (caution)
The sentence that decides it: "Like all scientists, he is cautious about interpreting behavior; scientists try to avoid rushing to conclusions." The passage names caution as the scientific virtue and then shows it — Marzluff won't say the gifts are intentional. Trained researchers and formal experiments are mentioned only as something he hopes for, not as what matters most.
Reading 15Technique used in lines 9–11marked Canswer: A
Lines 9–11 are pure numbers: 26,000 pounds of flour, 2,000,000 roti, 11,000 pounds of firewood. That's data illustrating the main idea (feeding 75,000 people is an enormous operation). Nothing earlier in the passage is being contradicted, which rules out (C).
Reading 17Yang includes all of the following EXCEPTmarked Canswer: E
The passage sorts them for you: yin = cool, soft, dark, still, feminine; yang = hot, hard, light, masculine, the sun, moving rivers. Darkness is yin, so it is the exception. The bowl line is explicit — "the inside space of a bowl (yin)… the outside of a bowl (yang)" — so (C) genuinely is yang.
Reading 18Best title for the passagemarked Eanswer: C
The passage defines yin, defines yang, gives examples, and explains their balance. That is Basic Principles of Yin and Yang. "The Yin-Yang In You" sounds fun but the passage isn't addressed to the reader personally. A title has to cover the whole passage, not its catchiest sentence.
Reading 19Function of the last paragraphmarked Danswer: A
The last paragraph says yin and yang are interdependent, inseparable, two sides of one thing, and that harmony is the ideal state. That is a wrap-up — it highlights the central ideas. "Inspire readers to notice" would need the paragraph to speak to the reader, and it doesn't.
Reading 20What can be inferredmarked Danswer: A
"these energies fluctuate over time… an energetic puppy who runs around frantically (yang), then suddenly falls asleep (yin)" — that is exactly yin becoming yang and back. Traditional Chinese medicine is never mentioned anywhere in the passage. An inference must still be built from words on the page, not from what you already know.
Reading 21"intertwined" (line 6) most likely meansmarked Danswer: A
"Inside each half is a dot of the opposite color, which illustrates how yin and yang are intertwined." The dots show the two are connected — each lives inside the other. "Twisted" is the literal picture of twine, but a dot isn't twisted around anything.
Reading 22Primary purpose of the Mount St. Helens passagemarked Canswer: B

Reading 24According to the passage, tectonic platesmarked Aanswer: C

Reading 27"Like a great throne" is an example ofmarked Canswer: D
The word "like" is the giveaway: a comparison using like or as is a simile. Irony would mean the poet meant the opposite of what he said, and he doesn't.
Reading 30Lines 16–18 most nearly meanmarked Eanswer: B

Section 4 · Verbal — what went wrong

Every miss, with the trap named

Synonym 2SLEEKmarked Canswer: A (suave)
Sleek means smooth and glossy; used of a person it means smooth and polished — suave. "Improper" is close to the opposite of polished.
Synonym 10ACCUMULATEmarked Banswer: E (accrue)
Accumulate = to build up in a pile over time. Accrue is the exact match (interest accrues). "Mix" is combining things, which isn't the same as growing.
Synonym 14EXERTIONmarked Canswer: D (effort)
Think of the verb: to exert yourself is to put out force, so exertion is effort. A pastime is a hobby — the pleasant opposite of exertion.
Synonym 15INCLINEmarked Canswer: D (slope)
An incline is a slope (a steep incline). "Hover" means to float in place, which has no slant in it at all.
Synonym 17IGNORANTmarked Eanswer: D (uninformed)
Ignorant comes from ignore — it means not knowing, i.e. uninformed. "Foolish" is an insult about sense, not about knowledge. The SSAT loves this trap: pick the definition, not the insult the word is usually used with.
Synonym 18PUNGENTmarked Banswer: C (aromatic)
Pungent describes a sharp, strong smell or taste (pungent cheese). Greasy is about texture. Ask yourself which sense the word belongs to.
Synonym 22RESIDENTmarked Danswer: C (tenant)
To reside is to live somewhere, so a resident is someone who lives there — a tenant. A foreigner is someone from elsewhere, nearly the opposite.
Synonym 26DIVISIVEmarked Canswer: B (disruptive)
Divisive means causing division between people (a divisive argument). "Breakable" comes from reading "divide" as splitting an object — but divisive is about groups, not things.
Synonym 27EXHAUSTIVEmarked Aanswer: C (thorough)
Exhaustive = covering everything (an exhaustive search). Exhausting = draining. One letter apart, completely different meanings — read the ending carefully.
Synonym 30STAMINAmarked Aanswer: D (perseverance)
Stamina is staying power — the ability to keep going. That's perseverance. "Pace" is how fast you go, not how long you last.
Analogy 33Torment : bothermarked Danswer: E (douse : dampen)
The bridge is intensity: to torment is an extreme version of to bother. To douse (soak completely) is an extreme version of to dampen (make slightly wet). Tremble and shake are about equally strong, so there's no jump. Build the sentence "___ is an extreme form of ___" and test each pair.
Analogy 36Volunteer : force ⇒ request : ___marked Banswer: E (arrest)
The bridge is willing vs. compelled. You can volunteer to go, or you can be forced to go. You can be requested to come in — or you can be arrested and made to. "Steal" changes the subject to taking things instead of a person being made to act.
Analogy 39Contemplate : consider ⇒ worry : ___marked Danswer: B (fret)
Contemplate and consider are synonyms, so you need a synonym for worry: to fret. "Indifference" is not caring — the opposite of worry. Fix the relationship from the first pair before you look at the choices.
Analogy 44Garage : carmarked Eanswer: C (drawer : silverware)
A garage is where you store a car; a drawer is where you store silverware. A steering wheel is a part of a truck — a different relationship entirely.
Analogy 45Rice : grain ⇒ bean : ___marked Banswer: E (legume)
Rice is a type of grain; a bean is a type of legume. "Cuisine" is a style of cooking, not a category a bean belongs to.
Analogy 50Reporter : notepadmarked Eanswer: C (player : ball)
The bridge is the person and the tool of their trade — a reporter works with a notepad, a player works with a ball. "Painter : canvas" is close and it's a reasonable thought, but a canvas is the surface the work ends up on, not the tool in the hand. When two choices both look right, make the bridge sentence stricter until only one survives.

What to work on this week

  1. Never leave a bubble empty. A blank is a guaranteed 0; a guess costs only −¼ and pays +1 if it lands. Eliminate one choice, then commit. This is worth more points than any other single change.
  2. Watch the clock in thirds. 25 math questions in 30 minutes is a bit over a minute each. If a question has eaten 90 seconds, mark it, move on, and come back. Section 5 was lost to the clock, not to the math.
  3. Reading: point to the line. For every reading answer, find the sentence that proves it. If you can't, cross that choice off. Watch especially for EXCEPT questions — tick the four you can find, and the leftover is the answer.
  4. Reading: stay inside the line numbers. When a question says "lines 16–18", the answer lives in those three lines — not in the poem's overall moral.
  5. Math: check the second-to-last step. "Twice a day", "minus a negative", "divide by the original". Circle the tricky word in the question before you compute.
  6. Ten words for the vocab trainer: sleek, accrue, exertion, incline, ignorant, pungent, resident, divisive, exhaustive, stamina. Add exhaustive vs exhausting as a pair — that one shows up constantly.
  7. Analogies: say the bridge out loud. One clear sentence linking the first two words, then test each choice against that exact sentence.
Bottom line: your math brain is in good shape and your analogy reasoning is genuinely strong. The score isn't being lost to difficulty — it's being lost to blanks and to reading answers that were never on the page. Those are both habits, and habits move fast.
