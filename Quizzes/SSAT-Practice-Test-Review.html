🔗 Section 3 · Analogies

9 missed of 30 · build the bridge before you look at the choices

The bridge method — use it on every analogy. Before you read a single answer choice, turn the first pair into one clear sentence:

Spoke is to wheel → "A spoke is one of many identical bars repeated inside a wheel."

Then read the choices and say the same sentence with the new words in it. Only one will survive. If two survive, your bridge was too loose — make it more specific and try again. Every analogy you missed was a loose-bridge mistake, not a vocabulary mistake.

🎯 Four habits that fix most of this

  1. Stop leaving blanks. You left 15 questions empty — 11 verbal, 4 reading. The SSAT takes off only ¼ point for a wrong answer and 0 for a blank, so the moment you can cross out even one choice, guessing wins. On the words you truly don't know, cross out the obvious opposite first, then pick. Filling in those 15 with a one-choice-eliminated guess is worth roughly +3 raw points on average — and much more if the root gives you a hint (iconic → icon, amicable → amigo).
  2. Beware the sound-alike. ANTICIPATE→participate, INTREPID→detailed (intricate), RAMPANT→ruined, "wrought"→wrung. Four misses came from a word that looked like the real one. Before you commit, say the word in a sentence you've actually heard. If you can't build one, it's probably not the word you think it is.
  3. Answer from the sentence, not the mood. "Mellow" felt dark because it was night; "critical" felt right because the writer once said "imperfect". Both times the actual sentence said something else. On any word-in-context or attitude question, put your finger on the line and read it out loud, then test each choice by plugging it in.
  4. Check who a detail belongs to. The toads sang the lullaby, not Solomon. The flowers are white and yellow, the butterfly is orange. Test writers build wrong answers out of true details attached to the wrong thing. One question — "whose is this?" — kills the trap.
