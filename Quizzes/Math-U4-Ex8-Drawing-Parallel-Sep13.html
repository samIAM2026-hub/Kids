🛝 The slide: drawing parallel lines

Drawing a parallel line needs both tools — the set-square and a ruler. The ruler is not for measuring; it is a rail for the set-square to slide along.

1
Lay one edge of the set-square along the given line.
2
Push the ruler up against a different edge of the set-square and hold the ruler still — it must not move again.
3
Slide the set-square along the ruler. Its first edge keeps pointing exactly the same way the whole time.
4
Draw along that first edge in its new place. The new line is parallel to the old one.

The commonest slip: letting the ruler shift. If the rail shifts or twists, the set-square turns, and the new line comes out at a slight angle instead of parallel.

📋 Filling in the table without missing a pair

Question 2 gives a figure full of lines and asks you to list every parallel pair and every perpendicular pair in a table. Do it in a fixed order and nothing gets lost:

1
Take the first line. Compare it with every other line in turn, writing down // or ⊥ when one applies.
2
Move to the second line and do the same, but skip anything you already wrote from the first.
3
Carry on until the last line. Every pair has then been checked exactly once.

Some pairs may be neither — not parallel and not perpendicular. That is a fine answer: those pairs simply do not go in the table.

🤝 How the two kinds fit together

In a figure built from rectangles, the two relations come as a package:

if AB // CD and CD ⊥ EF, then AB ⊥ EF

Parallel lines both make the same angle with anything that crosses them, so anything perpendicular to one of a parallel pair is perpendicular to the other too. Spotting that saves you half the checking.
